\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Multivariate predictive modeling in mass spectrometry aims to construct generalizable molecular signatures for patient stratification, disease diagnosis, and therapeutic response forecasting. Quantitative mass spectrometry operates in the high-dimensional regime where quantified features outnumber sample cohorts by one to three orders of magnitude ($p \gg n$; frequently $p \sim 10^3$--$10^5$ versus $n \sim 10^1$--$10^2$). In this geometry, ordinary least squares estimators are singular, and empirical classification accuracy can reach 100\% on random noise through geometric over-fitting.

This chapter details the statistical foundations and computational workflows required to train and validate machine learning models in mass spectrometry. We examine the geometric properties of high-dimensional Euclidean space, the Curse of Dimensionality, and Cover's theorem on linear separability. We derive the analytical properties of penalized loss functions, focusing on the Elastic Net and its grouping bound for collinear peptide and metabolite clusters. We analyze non-linear ensemble architectures, including Random Forests and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost), compare Gini impurity against game-theoretic SHAP feature importance, and address Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). We establish resampling protocols to prevent data leakage, covering nested cross-validation, class-imbalance corrections, probability calibration (Brier score, Expected Calibration Error, and Platt scaling), and clinical decision curve analysis. The chapter provides an executable R laboratory using the \texttt{tidymodels} framework, the Five Deadly Sins of omics machine learning, a diagnostic checklist, and worked exercises with solutions.
\end{abstract}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Tutor's Methodological Warning: The Data Leakage Catastrophe}\\
Data leakage occurs when information from outside the training dataset influences model training \cite{ambroise2002selection, broadhurst2006statistical}. If you normalize, impute missing values, or screen features via univariate two-sample tests (even with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate control~\cite{benjamini1995controlling}) across all $N$ patients before cross-validation splitting, information from the validation test fold leaks into the training pipeline. The resulting 98\% accuracy is an artifact of leakage that drops toward chance on independent clinical cohorts. All preprocessing steps must remain encapsulated strictly within the resampling training fold \cite{kuhn2022tidymodels}.
\end{notebox}

\section{The High-Dimensional Pathology (\texorpdfstring{$p \gg n$}{p >> n}) in Mass Spectrometry}
% =============================================================================

Clinical proteomics cohorts quantify $p = 4{,}000$ to $10{,}000$ proteins, and untargeted metabolomics profiles thousands of spectral features across $n = 50$ to $200$ patients. This condition violates the asymptotic assumptions of classical multivariate statistics.

\subsection{The Curse of Dimensionality and Geometric Sparsity}

As dimension $p$ increases, Euclidean geometry exhibits two non-intuitive behaviors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Hypersphere Volume Vanishing:} The volume of a $p$-dimensional hypersphere of radius $r$ inscribed within a hypercube of side length $2r$ is $V_p(r) = \frac{\pi^{p/2}}{\Gamma(p/2 + 1)} r^p$. The ratio of the sphere volume to the enclosing hypercube $(2r)^p$ converges asymptotically to zero: $\lim_{p \to \infty} \frac{V_p(r)}{(2r)^p} = \lim_{p \to \infty} \frac{\pi^{p/2}}{2^p \, \Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2} + 1\right)} = 0$ \cite{hastie2009elements}. Almost all volume concentrates in the corners of the hypercube.
    
    \item \textbf{Distance Concentration:} For $n$ points drawn independently in $\mathbb{R}^p$, pairwise distances concentrate around the mean: $\lim_{p \to \infty} \frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\max} - \operatorname{dist}_{\min}}{\operatorname{dist}_{\min}} = 0$. Sample pairs become equidistant, causing unpenalized nearest-neighbor algorithms to degenerate.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cover's Theorem on Separability}

Consider $n$ random data points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^p$, each assigned a binary label $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ uniformly at random. By Cover's theorem~\cite{cover1965geometrical}, the probability $P(n, p)$ that these points are linearly separable by a hyperplane is:
\begin{equation}
P(n, p) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1} \sum_{k=0}^{p} \binom{n-1}{k}
\end{equation}
Whenever $p \ge n - 1$, the summation encompasses all terms $0 \le k \le n-1$, giving $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \binom{n-1}{k} = 2^{n-1} \implies P(n, p) = 1.0$.

\begin{biologicalinsight}[top=2pt, bottom=2pt, before skip=2pt, after skip=2pt]
\textbf{The Inevitability of 100\% Training Accuracy:}\\
When features outnumber samples ($p > n$), an unregularized linear classifier achieves 100\% training accuracy on Gaussian noise with randomly permuted labels. High training accuracy in $p \gg n$ data is not evidence of biomarker discovery; it is a mathematical property of the linear system's null space \cite{cover1965geometrical, vapnik1998statistical}.
\end{biologicalinsight}

\subsection{Batch Effects, Confounder Leakage, and Group-Aware Resampling}

Instrument drift, column aging, ambient temperature variation, and reagent lots generate technical batch effects in mass spectrometry assays \cite{broadhurst2006statistical}. When batch normalization algorithms (such as ComBat or empirical Bayes scaling) are fitted across the pooled cohort before splitting, estimated location and scale parameters borrow variance across partition boundaries. This leaks test partition outcome information into the training data, inflating validation accuracy on pure noise. When biological classes correlate with acquisition batches or run order, classifiers memorize batch-specific background ions rather than disease markers. Preprocessing pipelines must estimate batch adjustment parameters strictly on the training partition and apply frozen transformations to validation folds. In multi-site or multi-day studies, models must be evaluated using Leave-One-Batch-Out Cross-Validation (LOBOCV) or stratified Group K-Fold cross-validation to assess true cross-batch generalization.

\section{Theoretical Foundations of Mass Spectrometry Machine Learning}
% =============================================================================

To prevent over-fitting in the $p \gg n$ regime, we introduce inductive biases through regularized loss functions, latent subspace projections, and non-linear tree ensembles.

\subsection{Penalized Estimation: Ridge, LASSO, and the Elastic Net}

For binary classification with labels $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and standardized feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$, the penalized logistic regression objective minimizes the empirical negative log-likelihood plus a penalty functional $\mathcal{P}_\alpha(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ \cite{hoerl1970ridge, tibshirani1996regression}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:penalized_objective}
\min_{\beta_0, \boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ y_i \ln \pi(\mathbf{x}_i) + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - \pi(\mathbf{x}_i)) \right] + \lambda \, \mathcal{P}_\alpha(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \right\}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(\mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(\beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_i^T \boldsymbol{\beta}))}$.

Zou and Hastie~\cite{zou2005regularization} formulated the Elastic Net penalty as a convex combination of $\ell_1$ (LASSO \cite{tibshirani1996regression}) and $\ell_2$ (Ridge \cite{hoerl1970ridge}) penalties:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_net_penalty}
\mathcal{P}_\alpha(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \alpha \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_1 + \frac{1 - \alpha}{2} \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^p \left( \alpha |\beta_j| + \frac{1 - \alpha}{2} \beta_j^2 \right)
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ dictates shrinkage intensity, and $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ governs penalty geometry ($\alpha = 0$ is Ridge; $\alpha = 1$ is LASSO; $0 < \alpha < 1$ is Elastic Net).

Because the logistic negative log-likelihood is non-quadratic, Friedman, Hastie, and Tibshirani~\cite{friedman2010regularization} formulate coordinate descent within Penalized Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (PIRLS). At each outer iteration, a two-term Taylor expansion around current parameter estimates $(\tilde{\beta}_0, \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}})$ forms a weighted quadratic surrogate with working response $z_i$ and observation weights $w_i$:
\begin{equation}
z_i = \tilde{\eta}_i + \frac{y_i - \pi(\mathbf{x}_i)}{\pi(\mathbf{x}_i)(1 - \pi(\mathbf{x}_i))}, \quad w_i = \pi(\mathbf{x}_i)(1 - \pi(\mathbf{x}_i))
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\eta}_i = \tilde{\beta}_0 + \mathbf{x}_i^T \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is the current linear predictor. Cyclical coordinate descent then updates each coordinate $j \in \{1, \dots, p\}$ via the weighted soft-thresholding operator:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta}_j = \frac{\mathcal{S}\left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_{ij}(z_i - \tilde{z}_i^{(-j)}), \; \lambda \alpha \right)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_{ij}^2 + \lambda(1 - \alpha)}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{z}_i^{(-j)} = \tilde{\beta}_0 + \sum_{k \ne j} x_{ik}\tilde{\beta}_k$, and $\mathcal{S}(u, \gamma) = \operatorname{sign}(u) \max(0, |u| - \gamma)$. When the partial correlation $|u| \le \lambda \alpha$, coefficient $\hat{\beta}_j$ is set identically to zero.

\begin{deepdive}[top=3pt, bottom=3pt, before skip=3pt, after skip=3pt]
\textbf{Mathematical Proof of the Elastic Net Grouping Bound:}\\
Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ be the Elastic Net minimizer for normalized predictors $\mathbf{x}_j$ and $\mathbf{x}_k$ with sample correlation $\rho = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_j^T \mathbf{x}_k$. Assume $\operatorname{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \operatorname{sign}(\hat{\beta}_k) > 0$.
From the sub-gradient optimality conditions of Eq.~(\ref{eq:penalized_objective}):
\begin{align}
-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_j^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) + \lambda \alpha + \lambda(1 - \alpha)\hat{\beta}_j &= 0 \\
-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_k^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) + \lambda \alpha + \lambda(1 - \alpha)\hat{\beta}_k &= 0
\end{align}
Subtracting the second equality from the first:
\begin{equation}
\lambda(1 - \alpha)(\hat{\beta}_j - \hat{\beta}_k) = \frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_k)^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
\end{equation}
Applying Cauchy-Schwarz with standardized vectors ($\|\mathbf{x}_j\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{x}_k\|_2^2 = n$ and $\|\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_k\|_2 = \sqrt{2n(1 - \rho)}$):
\begin{equation}
|\hat{\beta}_j - \hat{\beta}_k| \le \frac{\sqrt{2(1 - \rho)}}{\lambda(1 - \alpha)} \cdot \text{RMSE}
\label{eq:grouping_bound}
\end{equation}
As correlation $\rho \to 1$, the upper bound converges to zero. Correlated biomarkers receive near-identical coefficients, preventing arbitrary feature drops \cite{zou2005regularization}.
\end{deepdive}

\subsection{Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA, OPLS-DA) and the Overfitting Trap}

Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis projects high-dimensional predictors $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ onto a low-dimensional latent subspace that maximizes covariance with class labels $\mathbf{Y}$ \cite{barker2003partial}:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{q}} \operatorname{cov}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}) \quad \text{subject to } \|\mathbf{w}\|_2 = 1, \; \|\mathbf{q}\|_2 = 1
\end{equation}
Trygg and Wold~\cite{trygg2002orthogonal} developed Orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA) to separate predictive from orthogonal variation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{T}_p \mathbf{P}_p^T + \mathbf{T}_o \mathbf{P}_o^T + \mathbf{E}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{T}_p \mathbf{P}_p^T$ isolates variation correlated with $\mathbf{Y}$, and $\mathbf{T}_o \mathbf{P}_o^T$ captures variation orthogonal to $\mathbf{Y}$, such as analytical drift or patient demographic covariates. In binary problems, OPLS-DA condenses the predictive variation onto a single score vector $\mathbf{t}_p$, enabling biomarker visualization through S-plots that map feature covariance against correlation.

Neither PLS-DA nor OPLS-DA prevents geometric over-fitting in $p \gg n$ settings \cite{broadhurst2006statistical, westerhuis2008assessment}. Because $\mathbf{Y}$ guides the orthogonal projection, OPLS-DA strips non-predictive variance out of the primary component, separating pure Gaussian noise into distinct visual clusters. The goodness-of-fit metric $R^2_Y$ is inflated by noise. Models must be validated using cross-validated predictive ability ($Q^2_Y$) and non-parametric response permutation testing ($N \ge 1{,}000$ label permutations) \cite{westerhuis2008assessment}.

\subsection{Tree Ensembles: Random Forest and Gradient Tree Boosting}

When biological relationships involve non-linear thresholds or epistatic interactions, decision tree ensembles provide effective predictive performance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} Breiman~\cite{breiman2001random} averages $B$ de-correlated decision trees constructed via bootstrap aggregation (bagging) and random feature sub-sampling ($\sqrt{p}$ candidate features per split). For individual tree variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise tree correlation $\rho$, ensemble variance is:
    \begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(\bar{T}) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\sigma^2
    \end{equation}
    As $B \to \infty$, variance converges to $\rho \sigma^2$. Random feature selection reduces $\rho$, lowering overall prediction variance without increasing bias.
    \item \textbf{Gradient Tree Boosting (XGBoost):} Chen and Guestrin~\cite{chen2016xgboost} extended Friedman's gradient boosting framework~\cite{friedman2001greedy} by optimizing a regularized objective via second-order Taylor expansion. At boosting round $t$, the objective for tree $f_t$ with $T_{\text{leaves}}$ terminal regions and leaf weights $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{leaves}}}$ is:
    \begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ g_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)^2 \right] + \gamma T_{\text{leaves}} + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{T_{\text{leaves}}} w_j^2
    \end{equation}
    where $f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) = w_{q(\mathbf{x}_i)}$ with leaf assignment function $q: \mathbb{R}^p \to \{1, \dots, T_{\text{leaves}}\}$, $g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}} \ell(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)})$, and $h_i = \partial^2_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}} \ell(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)})$. Regularization parameter $\gamma$ penalizes leaf proliferation, and $\lambda$ shrinks leaf weights.
\end{itemize}

In untargeted metabolomics feature tables ($p = 20{,}000$ to $100{,}000$), evaluating $m_{\text{try}} \approx \sqrt{p}$ candidate features per split encounters a split candidate dilution problem. When true biomarkers are sparse, candidate subsets rarely contain informative features, forcing individual decision trees to split on noise. This inflates individual tree error variance $\sigma^2$ and degrades ensemble accuracy. Exact TreeSHAP computes Shapley values in polynomial time $O(T_{\text{trees}} L_{\text{leaves}} D^2)$, where $D$ is tree depth and $L_{\text{leaves}}$ is maximum leaf count \cite{lundberg2020local}. In contrast, cyclical coordinate descent for Elastic Net requires $O(n p)$ operations per full coordinate sweep across all $p$ features, making penalized linear models efficient for high-dimensional screening. When non-linear ensembles are required on massive datasets, unsupervised variance filtering or principal component extraction must be executed strictly inside the resampling recipe before tree construction.

\subsection{Interpretability: Gini Impurity Selection Bias vs Game-Theoretic SHAP}

In clinical biomarker translation, identifying feature contributions is essential:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gini Impurity Bias:} Classical Random Forest variable importance measures the total decrease in node impurity (Gini index). Strobl et al.~\cite{strobl2007bias} proved that Gini importance is biased toward continuous features with high variance and multiple split points, over-ranking noise variables.
    \item \textbf{Game-Theoretic SHAP:} Lundberg and Lee~\cite{lundberg2017unified} applied cooperative game theory to model interpretation. The Shapley value $\phi_j$ assigns a unique additive attribution to feature $j$ across all possible feature subsets $S \subseteq F \setminus \{j\}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|! \, (|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \left[ f(S \cup \{j\}) - f(S) \right]
    \end{equation}
    TreeSHAP computes exact Shapley attributions in polynomial time $O(T L D^2)$, satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity axioms \cite{lundberg2020local}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Probability Calibration and Clinical Decision Curve Analysis}

Clinical decisions depend on calibrated disease probabilities $\hat{p}_i \in [0, 1]$ rather than hard binary labels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brier Score and Calibration:} The Brier score measures mean squared probability error:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{BS} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{p}_i)^2
    \end{equation}
    Under Murphy's decomposition, $\text{BS} = \text{Reliability} - \text{Resolution} + \text{Uncertainty}$. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid transformation to map uncalibrated scores to empirical probabilities \cite{platt1999probabilistic}:
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_i^{\text{cal}} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(A \hat{f}_i + B)}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Decision Curve Analysis (DCA):} Vickers and Elkin~\cite{vickers2006decision} introduced net benefit analysis:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Net Benefit}(p_t) = \frac{\text{True Positives}}{N} - \frac{\text{False Positives}}{N} \left( \frac{p_t}{1 - p_t} \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $p_t$ is the clinical decision threshold probability. DCA directly benchmarks the clinical net utility of the mass spectrometry signature against treat-all and treat-none strategies across clinical thresholds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Hands-On R Laboratory: Tidymodels Workflow in Proteomics}
% =============================================================================

We now implement an end-to-end, multi-model machine learning pipeline using the modern \texttt{tidymodels} framework \cite{kuhn2022tidymodels}. All code uses base R pipe syntax (\texttt{|>}) and is fully reproducible.

\begin{rchunk}{Simulating High-Dimensional Proteomics Cohort ($p \gg n$) with MAR and Batch Effect}
suppressPackageStartupMessages({
  library(tidymodels)
  library(glmnet)
  library(ranger)
  library(xgboost)
  library(themis)
  library(probably)
  library(dcurves)
  library(dplyr)
  library(ggplot2)
})

set.seed(2026)
n_samples  <- 120
n_proteins <- 4000L

status <- factor(c(rep("Control", 80), rep("Tumor", 40)), levels = c("Control", "Tumor"))

# Observed continuous covariate: Age drives MAR missingness
age_years <- c(rnorm(80, mean = 55, sd = 12), rnorm(40, mean = 58, sd = 12))
age_years <- pmax(18, pmin(90, age_years))

# Confounded acquisition batch assignment
assign_batch <- function(status_vec, seed = 2026) {
  set.seed(seed)
  batches <- character(length(status_vec))
  for (i in seq_along(status_vec)) {
    probs <- if (status_vec[i] == "Control") c(0.40, 0.25, 0.18, 0.10, 0.07) else c(0.05, 0.10, 0.18, 0.27, 0.40)
    batches[i] <- sample(LETTERS[1:5], size = 1, prob = probs)
  }
  factor(batches, levels = LETTERS[1:5])
}
batch_vec <- assign_batch(as.character(status))

X <- matrix(rnorm(n_samples * n_proteins, mean = 20, sd = 1.0), 
            nrow = n_samples, ncol = n_proteins)
colnames(X) <- paste0("PROT_", sprintf("%04d", 1:n_proteins))

# Module 1: Collinear co-regulated signature (Proteins 1-8, r ~ 0.70)
latent_driver <- c(rep(0, 80), rep(0.65, 40))
for (j in 1:8) {
  X[, j] <- latent_driver + rnorm(n_samples, mean = 20, sd = 0.8)
}

# Module 2: Epistatic interaction (Proteins 9 & 10, zero main effect)
tumor_idx <- 81:120
X[, 9]  <- X[, 9]  - 20
X[, 10] <- X[, 10] - 20
interaction_driver <- rnorm(40, mean = 1.5, sd = 0.3)
X[tumor_idx, 9]  <- X[tumor_idx, 9]  + 0.6 * interaction_driver
X[tumor_idx, 10] <- X[tumor_idx, 10] + 0.6 * interaction_driver

# Inject technical batch effects on non-marker background proteins (PROT_0021 to PROT_0220)
batch_shifts <- c(A = -0.75, B = -0.35, C = 0.00, D = 0.35, E = 0.75)
for (i in 1:n_samples) {
  b <- as.character(batch_vec[i])
  X[i, 21:220] <- X[i, 21:220] + batch_shifts[b]
}

# MAR missingness driven by Age
miss_prob <- plogis(-3.2 + 0.05 * (age_years - 55))
miss_mask <- matrix(runif(n_samples * n_proteins) < rep(miss_prob, times = n_proteins),
                    nrow = n_samples, ncol = n_proteins)

# MNAR component on low-abundance proteins near detection limit
low_abund <- which(colMeans(X, na.rm = TRUE) < 19.5)
for (j in low_abund) {
  thresh_j <- quantile(X[, j], 0.05, na.rm = TRUE)
  miss_mask[, j] <- miss_mask[, j] | ((X[, j] < thresh_j) & (runif(n_samples) < 0.30))
}
X[miss_mask] <- NA

df_cohort <- as.data.frame(X)
df_cohort$Status <- status
df_cohort$Age    <- age_years
df_cohort$Batch  <- batch_vec

# Stratified 75/25 Train-Test Split
set.seed(42)
split_obj  <- initial_split(df_cohort, prop = 0.75, strata = Status)
train_data <- training(split_obj)
test_data  <- testing(split_obj)

cat(sprintf("Simulated cohort: %d samples x %d features (p/n ratio ~ %.0f).\n",
            nrow(df_cohort), ncol(df_cohort) - 3, (ncol(df_cohort) - 3) / nrow(df_cohort)))
cat(sprintf("Training set: %d patients (%d Control, %d Tumor)\n",
            nrow(train_data), sum(train_data$Status == "Control"), sum(train_data$Status == "Tumor")))
cat(sprintf("Independent test holdout: %d patients (%d Control, %d Tumor)\n",
            nrow(test_data), sum(test_data$Status == "Control"), sum(test_data$Status == "Tumor")))

# MAR audit across age quartiles
train_data$.age_quartile <- cut(train_data$Age,
  breaks = quantile(train_data$Age, c(0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1)),
  include.lowest = TRUE, labels = c("Q1(young)", "Q2", "Q3", "Q4(old)"))
protein_cols <- grep("^PROT_", names(train_data), value = TRUE)
miss_by_age <- train_data |> group_by(.age_quartile) |>
  summarise(missingness_rate = round(mean(is.na(as.matrix(across(all_of(protein_cols))))), 4),
            n = n(), .groups = "drop")
print(miss_by_age)
train_data$.age_quartile <- NULL
\end{rchunk}

Listing 23.2 balances classes using \texttt{step\_downsample} with an under-sampling ratio of 1.2. Downsampling discards 24 control samples, reducing the training set from 90 to 66 patients. In small cohorts, discarding samples reduces statistical power. Synthetic over-sampling via \texttt{themis::step\_smote} generates artificial minority cases by interpolating between $k$-nearest neighbors; SMOTE must execute strictly inside the cross-validation training fold to prevent leakage (Deadly Sin 3). A third strategy is cost-sensitive loss weighting, which retains all training samples without synthesizing artificial points. In XGBoost, setting \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight = 2.0} doubles the loss penalty on misclassified tumor cases. In \texttt{ranger}, passing \texttt{case\_weights} prioritizes minority observations during split evaluation without altering sample geometry. When scaling to untargeted metabolomics ($p \ge 10^4$), unsupervised filtering (such as \texttt{step\_nzv} or variance thresholding) should precede model fitting in the recipe to reduce feature dimension without label leakage.

On small holdout cohorts ($N = 30$), marginal performance metrics carry wide estimation uncertainty. Listing~23.8 computes non-parametric bootstrap percentile confidence intervals ($B = 2{,}000$) alongside paired bootstrap difference tests ($\Delta_{\text{AUC}} = \text{AUC}_{\text{model}_1} - \text{AUC}_{\text{model}_2}$) evaluated across the same 30 holdout patients. Stating that architectures are equivalent based on overlapping marginal confidence intervals is an inferential fallacy \cite{schenker2001judging}; paired resampling evaluates whether difference distributions exclude zero. Under paired bootstrap testing, Elastic Net outperformed Random Forest ($\text{mean } \Delta_{\text{AUC}} = +0.175$, 95\% CI $[+0.030, +0.381]$, paired $p = 0.014$) and XGBoost ($\text{mean } \Delta_{\text{AUC}} = +0.099$, 95\% CI $[+0.004, +0.240]$, paired $p = 0.024$). However, the wide marginal intervals (e.g., Random Forest AUC $[0.608, 0.963]$) emphasize that holdout sizes of $N = 30$ carry substantial sampling variability, precluding definitive architectural ranking without external validation cohorts \cite{delong1988comparing}.

\section{The Five Deadly Sins of Omics Machine Learning}
% =============================================================================

\begin{pitfallbox}[top=3pt, bottom=3pt, before skip=3pt, after skip=3pt]
\textbf{Deadly Sin 1: Pre-Filtering and Batch Correction Data Leakage.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Screening features via univariate tests or fitting batch normalization models (such as ComBat or RUV) across all $N$ patients before train/test splitting.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Validation set labels and variance parameters leak into the training pipeline, yielding 99\% cross-validation accuracy that fails on independent instrument runs \cite{ambroise2002selection, broadhurst2006statistical}.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Execute all feature filtering, scaling, imputation, and batch parameter fitting strictly inside the cross-validation training fold (\texttt{tidymodels::recipe}), and validate using Leave-One-Batch-Out Cross-Validation (LOBOCV) \cite{kuhn2022tidymodels}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}[top=3pt, bottom=3pt, before skip=3pt, after skip=3pt]
\textbf{Deadly Sin 2: Using Default LASSO ($\alpha=1$) on Strongly Collinear Omics Data.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Applying standard LASSO to co-eluting peptides or tightly co-regulated metabolites.\\
\textit{Consequence:} LASSO arbitrarily picks one feature and zeroes out all biological partners, leading to erratic signatures that vary between cross-validation folds \cite{zou2005regularization}.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Deploy the Elastic Net with $\alpha \in [0.2, 0.8]$ to enforce the grouping property (Eq.~\ref{eq:grouping_bound}).
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}[top=3pt, bottom=3pt, before skip=3pt, after skip=3pt]
\textbf{Deadly Sin 3: Applying SMOTE to the Entire Dataset Before Resampling.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Generating synthetic minority samples using SMOTE on the complete dataset before cross-validation.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Synthetic test points are derived directly from training neighbors, causing over-fitting.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Apply SMOTE strictly to the training fold within the resampling recipe (\texttt{themis::step\_smote}) \cite{chawla2002smote}, or use cost-sensitive loss weights (\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}) to avoid discarding data or synthesizing artificial points.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}[top=3pt, bottom=3pt, before skip=3pt, after skip=3pt]
\textbf{Deadly Sin 4: Reporting Unnested Cross-Validation Performance.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Tuning hyperparameters on a cross-validation grid and reporting the best-tuned performance as the model's true accuracy.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Hyperparameter tuning on validation folds introduces optimistic selection bias.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Deploy nested cross-validation ($5 \times 5$) or an independent untouched holdout test partition \cite{kuhn2013applied}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}[top=3pt, bottom=3pt, before skip=3pt, after skip=3pt]
\textbf{Deadly Sin 5: Trusting Uncalibrated Probabilities and Neglecting Clinical Net Benefit.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Interpreting raw Random Forest vote fractions or SVM margins as absolute clinical risk probabilities without auditing calibration.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Probabilities cluster away from 0 and 1, distorting clinical decision thresholds and risking harmful patient interventions.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Audit Brier scores, plot reliability diagrams, apply Platt scaling, and evaluate Decision Curve Analysis \cite{brier1950verification, vickers2006decision}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\section{Worked-Out Numerical Solutions}
% =============================================================================

\subsection*{Solution to Exercise 23.1: Elastic Net Grouping Bound}

\textbf{1. Upper Bound for $\rho = 0.98$:}\\
From Eq.~(\ref{eq:grouping_bound}):
$$|\hat{\beta}_1 - \hat{\beta}_2| \le \frac{\sqrt{2(1 - \rho)}}{\lambda(1 - \alpha)} \cdot \text{RMSE}$$
Given $\rho = 0.98$, $\lambda = 0.50$, $\alpha = 0.40$, $\text{RMSE} = 0.80$:
$$\sqrt{2(1 - 0.98)} = \sqrt{2(0.02)} = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20$$
$$\lambda(1 - \alpha) = 0.50(1 - 0.40) = 0.50(0.60) = 0.30$$
$$|\hat{\beta}_1 - \hat{\beta}_2| \le \frac{0.20}{0.30} \times 0.80 = \frac{2}{3} \times 0.80 \approx 0.533$$

\textbf{2. Upper Bound for $\rho = 0.50$:}\\
$$\sqrt{2(1 - 0.50)} = \sqrt{2(0.50)} = \sqrt{1.0} = 1.00$$
$$|\hat{\beta}_1 - \hat{\beta}_2| \le \frac{1.00}{0.30} \times 0.80 = 3.333 \times 0.80 \approx 2.667$$
\textbf{Biological Interpretation:} For correlated biomarkers ($\rho = 0.98$), the Elastic Net guarantees that coefficients cannot differ by more than $0.533$. When correlation drops to $\rho = 0.50$, the bound relaxes to $2.667$ ($5\times$ wider), allowing the model to decouple features with independent biological trajectories \cite{zou2005regularization}.

\subsection*{Solution to Exercise 23.2: Brier Score Computation}

\textbf{1. Brier Score of Classifier:}\\
Individual squared errors:
\begin{align*}
(y_1 - \hat{p}_1)^2 &= (0 - 0.10)^2 = 0.0100 \\
(y_2 - \hat{p}_2)^2 &= (0 - 0.30)^2 = 0.0900 \\
(y_3 - \hat{p}_3)^2 &= (1 - 0.60)^2 = 0.1600 \\
(y_4 - \hat{p}_4)^2 &= (1 - 0.85)^2 = 0.0225 \\
(y_5 - \hat{p}_5)^2 &= (1 - 0.90)^2 = 0.0100
\end{align*}
Sum of squared errors:
$$\sum_{i=1}^5 (y_i - \hat{p}_i)^2 = 0.0100 + 0.0900 + 0.1600 + 0.0225 + 0.0100 = 0.2925$$
$$\text{BS} = \frac{0.2925}{5} = 0.0585$$

\textbf{2. Comparison to Naive Benchmark:}\\
For $\hat{p} \equiv 0.50$:
$$(0 - 0.5)^2 \times 2 + (1 - 0.5)^2 \times 3 = 0.25 \times 5 = 1.25 \implies \text{BS}_{\text{naive}} = \frac{1.25}{5} = 0.2500$$
The trained classifier achieves a Brier score of $0.0585$, a $76.6\%$ reduction in prediction error compared to the uninformative baseline ($0.2500$), demonstrating probability calibration \cite{brier1950verification}.

\subsection*{Solution to Exercise 23.3: Decision Curve Analysis Net Benefit}

\textbf{1. Net Benefit of Proteomic Model at $p_t = 0.25$:}\\
From Vickers and Elkin~\cite{vickers2006decision}:
$$\text{Net Benefit} = \frac{\text{TP}}{N} - \frac{\text{FP}}{N}\left(\frac{p_t}{1 - p_t}\right)$$
Given $N = 100$, $\text{TP} = 24$, $\text{FP} = 12$, $p_t = 0.25$:
$$\frac{p_t}{1 - p_t} = \frac{0.25}{1 - 0.25} = \frac{0.25}{0.75} = \frac{1}{3}$$
$$\text{Net Benefit} = \frac{24}{100} - \frac{12}{100}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 0.240 - 0.040 = 0.200$$

\textbf{2. Net Benefit of Default "Treat All":}\\
If all 100 patients receive treatment, $\text{TP} = 30$ and $\text{FP} = 70$:
$$\text{Net Benefit}_{\text{All}} = \frac{30}{100} - \frac{70}{100}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 0.300 - 0.2333 = 0.0667$$

\textbf{3. Clinical Utility Comparison:}\\
For "Treat None", $\text{TP} = 0$ and $\text{FP} = 0$, giving $\text{Net Benefit}_{\text{None}} = 0.000$.
The model's net benefit of $0.200$ exceeds both Treat-All ($0.067$) and Treat-None ($0.000$). The net benefit gain over Treat-All is $0.200 - 0.067 = 0.133$, which corresponds to detecting $13.3$ additional true cancer cases per 100 patients without increasing false-positive interventions.
