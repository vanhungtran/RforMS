8  Annotate Metabolites from Feature Tables

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

“You have successfully processed 100 gigabytes of raw LC-MS data. Your statistical analysis reveals 42 features that are significantly upregulated in your disease cohort. But a feature is just a mathematical coordinate: m/z 205.097 at 4.2 minutes. What molecule is it? Until you answer that question, your biological narrative is stalled.”

In bottom-up proteomics, identification relies on a known, constrained search space: genome-derived protein sequences are digested in silico into predictable tryptic peptides. In contrast, untargeted metabolomics operates in a vast, open chemical space. The human metabolome comprises endogenous metabolites, dietary compounds, microbial transformations, and xenobiotics—numbering in the hundreds of thousands of distinct molecular entities.

Furthermore, electrospray ionization (ESI) does not generate a single clean peak per molecule. A single metabolite typically generates a constellation of signals: protonated molecules, alkali adducts ([M+\text{Na}]^+, [M+\text{K}]^+), ammonium adducts, isotopic envelopes (M+1, M+2), in-source fragments, and multimeric clusters. Conversely, a single measured mass often matches dozens of distinct isomeric structures (e.g., structural isomers like leucine and isoleucine, or stereoisomers like glucose and galactose).

This chapter covers the computational discipline of metabolite annotation: deconvoluting feature multiplets with CAMERA, applying chemical formulas and adduct arithmetic with MetaboCoreUtils, searching reference databases using modern MetaboAnnotation workflows, scoring isotopic envelopes, and reporting putative identifications according to rigorous international standards.

WarningThe One Mistake to Avoid

Reporting an exact-mass database match as a confirmed chemical identification. An accurate mass match alone is a hypothesis (MSI Level 4 or Level 3), not a definitive compound name. Never claim an identification without matching retention time against an authentic chemical standard or matching MS/MS fragmentation spectra against a validated reference library.

8.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • Classify metabolite identifications according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) confidence levels (Levels 1–5)
  • Explain the physical chemistry of electrospray adduct formation in positive and negative ionization modes
  • Deconvolve feature tables into pseudo-spectra containing grouped isotopes, adducts, and in-source fragments using CAMERA
  • Perform exact monoisotopic mass and adduct calculations using MetaboCoreUtils
  • Execute tolerance-aware exact-mass database queries with parts-per-million (ppm) error control
  • Model theoretical isotopic distributions and score experimental isotopic envelopes to eliminate false positive formulas
  • Differentiate structural and stereochemical isomers using orthogonal analytical dimensions (retention time, CCS, and MS/MS)
  • Synthesize annotated features into a structured SummarizedExperiment container ready for biological modeling

8.2 The Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Confidence Framework

In 2007, the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) established standard reporting criteria to prevent over-confident annotations in the literature (Sumner et al. 2007). This framework was further refined by Schymanski et al. (2014) and modernized by the international metabolomics community:

Level Classification Required Evidence Typical Confidence
Level 1 Confirmed Structure \bullet Accurate m/z (\le 5\text{ ppm})
\bullet Retention time match (\Delta\text{RT} \le 0.1\text{ min}) to an authentic standard on the same instrument
\bullet High-resolution MS/MS spectral similarity (cosine score \ge 0.85)		 Unequivocal structural identification
Level 2 Putatively Annotated Compound \bullet Accurate m/z (\le 5\text{ ppm})
\bullet MS/MS spectral library match to external public databases (MoNA, MassBank, GNPS, HMDB)
\bullet No authentic standard analyzed locally		 High confidence; potential isomer ambiguity
Level 3 Putatively Characterized Compound Class \bullet Characteristic MS/MS fragmentation motifs (e.g., diagnostic headgroup loss for phosphatidylcholines, characteristic aglycone neutral loss)
\bullet Exact mass consistent with chemical class		 Class known; specific regioisomer/stereoisomer unresolved
Level 4 Putative Chemical Formula \bullet Exact monoisotopic mass (\le 3\text{ ppm})
\bullet Isotopic fine-structure confirmation (M+1, M+2 abundance and mass spacing)
\bullet No spectral fragmentation available		 Elemental composition known; structure unknown
Level 5 Unknown Feature \bullet Repeatable chromatographic peak (m/z and retention time coordinate)
\bullet Unmatched to any database or formula		 Unidentified chromatographic feature

8.3 Electrospray Ionization and Adduct Chemistry

In electrospray ionization (ESI), neutral molecules (M) in solution are transferred into gas-phase ions through desolvation and charge transfer. Analytes acquire charge either through protonation/deprotonation or through coordination with mobile phase buffer ions and ubiquitous trace salts.

8.3.1 Positive Ionization Mode (\text{ESI}^+)

In positive mode, compounds with basic functional groups (amines, amides) or polar functional groups protonate or form metal coordination complexes:

\text{Protonated Molecule: } M + \text{H}^+ \implies [M+\text{H}]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 1.007276)

\text{Sodium Adduct: } M + \text{Na}^+ \implies [M+\text{Na}]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 22.989218)

\text{Potassium Adduct: } M + \text{K}^+ \implies [M+\text{K}]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 38.963158)

\text{Ammonium Adduct: } M + \text{NH}_4^+ \implies [M+\text{NH}_4]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 18.033826)

\text{Water Loss Fragment: } [M+\text{H}-\text{H}_2\text{O}]^+ \quad (m/z = M - 17.003289)

\text{Dimeric Ion: } 2M + \text{H}^+ \implies [2M+\text{H}]^+ \quad (m/z = 2M + 1.007276)

8.3.2 Negative Ionization Mode (\text{ESI}^-)

In negative mode, acidic compounds (carboxylic acids, sulfates, phosphates) deprotonate or bind anions:

\text{Deprotonated Molecule: } M - \text{H}^- \implies [M-\text{H}]^- \quad (m/z = M - 1.007276)

\text{Formate Adduct: } M + \text{HCOO}^- \implies [M+\text{HCOO}]^- \quad (m/z = M + 44.998201)

\text{Acetate Adduct: } M + \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- \implies [M+\text{CH}_3\text{COO}]^- \quad (m/z = M + 59.013851)

\text{Chloride Adduct: } M + \text{Cl}^- \implies [M+\text{Cl}]^- \quad (m/z = M + 34.969402)

If these adducts are not deconvoluted, a single biological metabolite will appear as 5–8 distinct “features” in the statistical matrix. If an intervention shifts this metabolite, all 8 features will be flagged as statistically significant in differential testing (Chapter 21), distorting false discovery rates and downstream pathway analysis.

8.4 Step 1: Deconvoluting Features with CAMERA

The Bioconductor package CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms for MEtabolite pRofile Annotation) deconvolutes raw feature tables by grouping co-eluting peaks into pseudo-spectra (components) and assigning isotopic and adduct relationships.

8.4.1 The Mathematical Principles of CAMERA:

  1. Retention Time Grouping (groupFWHM): Features are initially binned if their retention times fall within a fraction of their chromatographic Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM).
  2. EIC Peak Shape Correlation (groupCorr): Across all samples, extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for features in the same retention time window are correlated. If two features originate from the same parent metabolite (e.g., [M+\text{H}]^+ and [M+\text{Na}]^+), their chromatographic elution profiles and sample-to-sample abundance patterns exhibit high Pearson correlation (r \ge 0.75).
  3. Isotope Detection (findIsotopes): Peaks within a pseudo-spectrum are searched for characteristic carbon-13 mass differences (\Delta m \approx 1.003355\text{ Da}) and expected binomial intensity ratios.
  4. Adduct Annotation (findAdducts): Pairwise mass differences within the pseudo-spectrum are checked against a comprehensive library of known adduct transformations (e.g., [M+\text{Na}]^+ - [M+\text{H}]^+ = 21.98194\text{ Da}).
Code 
library(xcms)
library(CAMERA)

# Assume 'xdata' is an aligned and grouped XCMSnExp object from Chapter 7
# Convert to xcmsSet for CAMERA integration
xset <- as(xdata, "xcmsSet")

# Step 1: Initialize annotation container
xa <- xsAnnotate(xset)

# Step 2: Group features by chromatographic FWHM
xa <- groupFWHM(xa, perfwhm = 0.6)

# Step 3: Group by peak shape correlation across samples
xa <- groupCorr(
  xa, 
  cor_eic_th = 0.75,     # EIC correlation threshold
  calcIso    = TRUE,     # Use isotope information in grouping
  calcCiS    = TRUE,     # Correlation inside samples
  calcCaS    = TRUE      # Correlation across samples
)

# Step 4: Identify isotope peaks (mass tolerance = 0.01 Da / 10 ppm)
xa <- findIsotopes(xa, maxcharge = 3, maxiso = 4, ppm = 10, mzabs = 0.01)

# Step 5: Annotate adducts in positive ionization mode
xa <- findAdducts(
  xa, 
  polarity   = "positive", 
  max_peaks  = 100, 
  rules      = defaultChecklist$rules_pos
)

# Step 6: Extract the deconvoluted peak list
peaklist_cam <- getPeaklist(xa)

The resulting peaklist_cam table contains three critical diagnostic columns: - pcgroup: The pseudo-spectrum group ID. All features sharing a pcgroup originate from the same chemical entity. - isotopes: Identifies monoisotopic peaks [M], as well as [M+1], [M+2] isotopic peaks. - adduct: Specific assigned adduct formula (e.g., [M+Na]+, [M+K]+, [M+H-H2O]+).

Tip

By filtering the feature table to retain only the primary monoisotopic features (discarding [M+1] isotopes and redundant adducts for quantification), the dimensionality of the feature table is reduced by 60%–75%, dramatically mitigating the multiple-testing burden in Chapter 21.

8.5.3 Inferring Neutral Mass from Observed m/z

In untargeted workflows, the instrument measures an unknown peak at m/z = 203.0526. If chromatographic deconvolution identifies this feature as a sodium adduct ([M+\text{Na}]^+), we work in reverse using mz2mass() to calculate the neutral precursor mass:

observed_mz   <- 203.0526
inferred_mass <- mz2mass(observed_mz, adduct = "[M+Na]+")

8.6 Step 3: Tolerance-Aware Exact-Mass Database Searching

Because mass spectrometers measure with finite physical resolution, an observed mass must be matched against reference databases (HMDB, KEGG, ChEBI, LipidMaps) within an instrument-specific parts-per-million (ppm) window.

8.6.1 Mathematical Formulation of Mass Accuracy

Mass error is expressed in absolute Daltons (\Delta m) or relative parts-per-million (\text{ppm}):

\Delta m = |m_{\text{obs}} - m_{\text{theor}}|

\text{ppm error} = \frac{|m_{\text{obs}} - m_{\text{theor}}|}{m_{\text{theor}}} \times 10^6

The allowable search window [m_{\text{lower}}, m_{\text{upper}}] for a given tolerance \tau_{\text{ppm}} is:

m_{\text{lower}} = m_{\text{obs}} \left( 1 - \frac{\tau_{\text{ppm}}}{10^6} \right), \quad m_{\text{upper}} = m_{\text{obs}} \left( 1 + \frac{\tau_{\text{ppm}}}{10^6} \right)

8.6.2 Implementing Database Matching with dplyr Range Joins

Code 
library(dplyr)

# 1. Reference database snippet
ref_db <- tibble::tribble(
  ~compound_name,  ~formula,       ~exact_mass,
  "Glucose",       "C6H12O6",      180.063388,
  "Fructose",      "C6H12O6",      180.063388,  # Stereoisomer
  "Inositol",      "C6H12O6",      180.063388,  # Stereoisomer
  "Caffeine",      "C8H10N4O2",    194.080376,
  "Citric acid",   "C6H8O7",       192.027003
)

# 2. Experimental observed monoisotopic masses from LC-MS run
query_features <- tibble::tribble(
  ~feature_id, ~observed_mass, ~retention_time,
  "FT_001",    180.0639,       2.45,
  "FT_002",    194.0801,       6.12,
  "FT_003",    312.1245,       9.80   # Unmatched unknown
)

# Match within 5 ppm tolerance
ppm_tol <- 5

annotated_matches <- query_features %>%
  mutate(
    m_min = observed_mass * (1 - ppm_tol / 1e6),
    m_max = observed_mass * (1 + ppm_tol / 1e6)
  ) %>%
  cross_join(ref_db) %>%
  filter(exact_mass >= m_min & exact_mass <= m_max) %>%
  mutate(
    ppm_error = round(abs(observed_mass - exact_mass) / exact_mass * 1e6, 2)
  ) %>%
  select(feature_id, observed_mass, retention_time, compound_name, formula, ppm_error)

8.7 Step 4: Isotopic Pattern Simulation and Envelope Scoring

Exact mass alone is often insufficient to specify a single chemical formula. Above m/z = 300, dozens of distinct elemental formulas fall within a 5 ppm window.

To break formula degeneracy, high-resolution instruments measure the isotopic fine structure: the relative abundances and exact mass spacing of naturally occurring stable isotopes: - Carbon: ^{12}\text{C} (98.93\%), ^{13}\text{C} (1.07\%) - Nitrogen: ^{14}\text{N} (99.63\%), ^{15}\text{N} (0.37\%) - Oxygen: ^{16}\text{O} (99.76\%), ^{18}\text{O} (0.20\%) - Sulfur: ^{32}\text{S} (94.99\%), ^{34}\text{S} (4.21\%) - Chlorine: ^{35}\text{Cl} (75.76\%), ^{37}\text{Cl} (24.24\%)

The theoretical abundance of the M+1 isotope for a molecule with C carbon atoms is given by the binomial expansion:

\frac{I(M+1)}{I(M)} \approx C \times 1.07\% + N \times 0.37\%

A molecule containing sulfur or chlorine exhibits a prominent M+2 signature (^{34}\text{S} at +1.9958\text{ Da}, ^{37}\text{Cl} at +1.9970\text{ Da}).

Code 
# Inspect key isotope substitution mass shifts from MetaboCoreUtils
sub_rules <- MetaboCoreUtils::isotopicSubstitutionMatrix()
key_isotopes <- c("[13]C1", "[15]N1", "[18]O1", "[34]S1", "[37]Cl1")
unique_rules <- unique(sub_rules[sub_rules[, "name"] %in% key_isotopes, c("name", "md")])

data.frame(
  Isotope    = unique_rules[, "name"],
  Mass_Shift = round(unique_rules[, "md"], 6)
)

8.7.1 Isotope Pattern Similarity Metric

The similarity between an experimentally observed isotopic envelope \mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1, x_2) and a candidate theoretical distribution \mathbf{y} = (y_0, y_1, y_2) is quantified using the normalized dot product (cosine correlation):

S_{\text{iso}} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^k x_i y_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^k x_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^k y_i^2}}

Candidate formulas with S_{\text{iso}} < 0.90 are rejected as chemically implausible, dramatically pruning false positive annotations.

8.8 The Isomer Dilemma and Orthogonal Dimensions

In Chapter 8, FT_001 matched three distinct compounds: Glucose, Fructose, and Inositol. All share the identical formula \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 and exact mass 180.063388\text{ Da}.

Mass spectrometry alone cannot resolve stereoisomers without orthogonal separation:

+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                       Orthogonal Separation Dimensions                        |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. RETENTION TIME (RT) | Chromatographic interaction; requires local standard  |
| 2. ION MOBILITY (IMS)  | Collision Cross Section (CCS, A^2); gas-phase shape  |
| 3. TANDEM MS (MS/MS)   | Collision-induced dissociation; fragment fingerprints |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

8.8.1 Resolving Isomers:

  1. Retention Time Matching (\Delta\text{RT}): Authentic standards analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions allow discrimination between isomers. Glucose and galactose elute at distinct retention times on hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) columns.
  2. Ion Mobility Spectrometry (Collision Cross Section, CCS): Drift-tube and trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS) measure an ion’s rotational collision cross section (\text{CCS} in \text{\AA}^2). Structural isomers with different 3D shapes exhibit different drift times through a buffer gas.
  3. MS/MS Fragmentation: As shown in Chapter 10, isomers frequently generate distinct diagnostic fragment ions or fragment intensity ratios under controlled collision energies.

8.9 Synthesizing the Final Annotation Report

To prepare annotated data for statistical modeling, candidate matches must be folded into the rowData of a SummarizedExperiment object while preserving the rectangular integrity of the feature table:

Code 
# Collapse multiple candidate annotations into a delimited string
collapsed_report <- annotated_matches %>%
  group_by(feature_id, observed_mass, retention_time) %>%
  summarize(
    annotated_compounds = paste(compound_name, collapse = " | "),
    chemical_formulas   = paste(unique(formula), collapse = " | "),
    best_ppm_error      = min(ppm_error),
    match_count         = n(),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

# Join back with full feature table to classify unknowns
final_feature_metadata <- query_features %>%
  left_join(collapsed_report, by = c("feature_id", "observed_mass", "retention_time")) %>%
  mutate(
    annotated_compounds = ifelse(is.na(annotated_compounds), "Unknown", annotated_compounds),
    msi_confidence_level = case_when(
      annotated_compounds == "Unknown" ~ "Level 5 (Unknown)",
      match_count == 1 ~ "Level 3 (Putative Class / Match)",
      match_count > 1  ~ "Level 3 (Ambiguous Isomer Set)"
    )
  )

8.10 Common Pitfalls in Metabolite Annotation

Pitfall Consequence Recommended Practice
Assuming [M+\text{H}]^+ for all peaks If a feature is actually [M+\text{Na}]^+, the calculated neutral mass is incorrect by 21.98 Da, searching the database for an entirely wrong compound. Always run CAMERA or MetaboAnnotation deconvolution to confirm adduct identity before database matching.
Using wide mass tolerance (\ge 30\text{ ppm}) Produces hundreds of false positive database candidates per feature, rendering annotations useless. Restrict searches to instrument-verified mass accuracy (\le 5\text{ ppm} for Orbitrap/Q-TOF; calibrate externally).
Reporting Level 1 without authentic standards Violates MSI standards; leads to irreproducible biological narratives in peer-reviewed manuscripts. Restrict exact mass + MS/MS library hits to Level 2; reserve Level 1 for local authentic standard validation.
Ignoring in-source fragmentation Degradation products (e.g., neutral water loss, decarboxylation) are misidentified as novel biological metabolites. Check for co-eluting peaks with mass shifts corresponding to -\text{H}_2\text{O} (-18.01056\text{ Da}) or -\text{CO}_2 (-43.98983\text{ Da}).
Neglecting stereoisomers Fails to recognize that exact mass cannot differentiate hexose stereoisomers (glucose, fructose, galactose). Explicitly report ambiguous isomer sets in publications unless resolved by chromatography or authentic standards.

8.11 Summary

  • Metabolite annotation transforms anonymous m/z and retention time coordinates into biologically interpretable chemical structures.
  • The MSI confidence framework governs reporting: Level 1 (authentic standard), Level 2 (spectral library match), Level 3 (compound class), Level 4 (molecular formula), and Level 5 (unknown).
  • Electrospray ionization creates complex feature multiplet networks; CAMERA deconvolutes these into pseudo-spectra by correlating peak shapes (r \ge 0.75) and chromatographic retention times.
  • MetaboCoreUtils provides rigorous mathematical tools for exact mass calculations, adduct interconversions, and theoretical isotopic distributions.
  • Exact-mass database queries must control for relative parts-per-million (ppm) measurement error and should be validated using isotopic envelope scoring.
  • Orthogonal analytical dimensions—retention time matching, ion mobility collision cross sections (CCS), and MS/MS fragmentation—are required to resolve isomeric ambiguity.

# Annotate Metabolites from Feature Tables {#sec-annotate-metabolites}

> *"You have successfully processed 100 gigabytes of raw LC-MS data. Your statistical analysis reveals 42 features that are significantly upregulated in your disease cohort. But a feature is just a mathematical coordinate: $m/z$ 205.097 at 4.2 minutes. What molecule is it? Until you answer that question, your biological narrative is stalled."*

In bottom-up proteomics, identification relies on a known, constrained search space: genome-derived protein sequences are digested in silico into predictable tryptic peptides. In contrast, **untargeted metabolomics** operates in a vast, open chemical space. The human metabolome comprises endogenous metabolites, dietary compounds, microbial transformations, and xenobiotics—numbering in the hundreds of thousands of distinct molecular entities.

Furthermore, electrospray ionization (ESI) does not generate a single clean peak per molecule. A single metabolite typically generates a constellation of signals: protonated molecules, alkali adducts ($[M+\text{Na}]^+$, $[M+\text{K}]^+$), ammonium adducts, isotopic envelopes ($M+1, M+2$), in-source fragments, and multimeric clusters. Conversely, a single measured mass often matches dozens of distinct isomeric structures (e.g., structural isomers like leucine and isoleucine, or stereoisomers like glucose and galactose).

This chapter covers the computational discipline of metabolite annotation: deconvoluting feature multiplets with `CAMERA`, applying chemical formulas and adduct arithmetic with `MetaboCoreUtils`, searching reference databases using modern `MetaboAnnotation` workflows, scoring isotopic envelopes, and reporting putative identifications according to rigorous international standards.

::: {.callout-warning title="The One Mistake to Avoid"}
Reporting an exact-mass database match as a confirmed chemical identification. An accurate mass match alone is a *hypothesis* (MSI Level 4 or Level 3), not a definitive compound name. Never claim an identification without matching retention time against an authentic chemical standard or matching MS/MS fragmentation spectra against a validated reference library.
:::

## The Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Confidence Framework

In 2007, the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) established standard reporting criteria to prevent over-confident annotations in the literature [@sumner2007proposed]. This framework was further refined by Schymanski et al. (2014) and modernized by the international metabolomics community:

| Level | Classification | Required Evidence | Typical Confidence |
|:---:|:---|:---|:---|
| **Level 1** | **Confirmed Structure** | $\bullet$ Accurate $m/z$ ($\le 5\text{ ppm}$)<br>$\bullet$ Retention time match ($\Delta\text{RT} \le 0.1\text{ min}$) to an **authentic standard** on the same instrument<br>$\bullet$ High-resolution MS/MS spectral similarity (cosine score $\ge 0.85$) | Unequivocal structural identification |
| **Level 2** | **Putatively Annotated Compound** | $\bullet$ Accurate $m/z$ ($\le 5\text{ ppm}$)<br>$\bullet$ MS/MS spectral library match to external public databases (MoNA, MassBank, GNPS, HMDB)<br>$\bullet$ No authentic standard analyzed locally | High confidence; potential isomer ambiguity |
| **Level 3** | **Putatively Characterized Compound Class** | $\bullet$ Characteristic MS/MS fragmentation motifs (e.g., diagnostic headgroup loss for phosphatidylcholines, characteristic aglycone neutral loss)<br>$\bullet$ Exact mass consistent with chemical class | Class known; specific regioisomer/stereoisomer unresolved |
| **Level 4** | **Putative Chemical Formula** | $\bullet$ Exact monoisotopic mass ($\le 3\text{ ppm}$)<br>$\bullet$ Isotopic fine-structure confirmation ($M+1, M+2$ abundance and mass spacing)<br>$\bullet$ No spectral fragmentation available | Elemental composition known; structure unknown |
| **Level 5** | **Unknown Feature** | $\bullet$ Repeatable chromatographic peak ($m/z$ and retention time coordinate)<br>$\bullet$ Unmatched to any database or formula | Unidentified chromatographic feature |

## Electrospray Ionization and Adduct Chemistry

In electrospray ionization (ESI), neutral molecules ($M$) in solution are transferred into gas-phase ions through desolvation and charge transfer. Analytes acquire charge either through protonation/deprotonation or through coordination with mobile phase buffer ions and ubiquitous trace salts.

### Positive Ionization Mode ($\text{ESI}^+$)

In positive mode, compounds with basic functional groups (amines, amides) or polar functional groups protonate or form metal coordination complexes:

$$\text{Protonated Molecule: } M + \text{H}^+ \implies [M+\text{H}]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 1.007276)$$

$$\text{Sodium Adduct: } M + \text{Na}^+ \implies [M+\text{Na}]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 22.989218)$$

$$\text{Potassium Adduct: } M + \text{K}^+ \implies [M+\text{K}]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 38.963158)$$

$$\text{Ammonium Adduct: } M + \text{NH}_4^+ \implies [M+\text{NH}_4]^+ \quad (m/z = M + 18.033826)$$

$$\text{Water Loss Fragment: } [M+\text{H}-\text{H}_2\text{O}]^+ \quad (m/z = M - 17.003289)$$

$$\text{Dimeric Ion: } 2M + \text{H}^+ \implies [2M+\text{H}]^+ \quad (m/z = 2M + 1.007276)$$

### Negative Ionization Mode ($\text{ESI}^-$)

In negative mode, acidic compounds (carboxylic acids, sulfates, phosphates) deprotonate or bind anions:

$$\text{Deprotonated Molecule: } M - \text{H}^- \implies [M-\text{H}]^- \quad (m/z = M - 1.007276)$$

$$\text{Formate Adduct: } M + \text{HCOO}^- \implies [M+\text{HCOO}]^- \quad (m/z = M + 44.998201)$$

$$\text{Acetate Adduct: } M + \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- \implies [M+\text{CH}_3\text{COO}]^- \quad (m/z = M + 59.013851)$$

$$\text{Chloride Adduct: } M + \text{Cl}^- \implies [M+\text{Cl}]^- \quad (m/z = M + 34.969402)$$

If these adducts are not deconvoluted, a single biological metabolite will appear as 5–8 distinct "features" in the statistical matrix. If an intervention shifts this metabolite, all 8 features will be flagged as statistically significant in differential testing (Chapter 21), distorting false discovery rates and downstream pathway analysis.

## Step 1: Deconvoluting Features with CAMERA

The Bioconductor package `CAMERA` (Collection of Algorithms for MEtabolite pRofile Annotation) deconvolutes raw feature tables by grouping co-eluting peaks into **pseudo-spectra** (components) and assigning isotopic and adduct relationships.

### The Mathematical Principles of CAMERA:

1. **Retention Time Grouping (`groupFWHM`)**: Features are initially binned if their retention times fall within a fraction of their chromatographic Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM).
2. **EIC Peak Shape Correlation (`groupCorr`)**: Across all samples, extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for features in the same retention time window are correlated. If two features originate from the same parent metabolite (e.g., $[M+\text{H}]^+$ and $[M+\text{Na}]^+$), their chromatographic elution profiles and sample-to-sample abundance patterns exhibit high Pearson correlation ($r \ge 0.75$).
3. **Isotope Detection (`findIsotopes`)**: Peaks within a pseudo-spectrum are searched for characteristic carbon-13 mass differences ($\Delta m \approx 1.003355\text{ Da}$) and expected binomial intensity ratios.
4. **Adduct Annotation (`findAdducts`)**: Pairwise mass differences within the pseudo-spectrum are checked against a comprehensive library of known adduct transformations (e.g., $[M+\text{Na}]^+ - [M+\text{H}]^+ = 21.98194\text{ Da}$).

```{r}
#| eval: false
library(xcms)
library(CAMERA)

# Assume 'xdata' is an aligned and grouped XCMSnExp object from Chapter 7
# Convert to xcmsSet for CAMERA integration
xset <- as(xdata, "xcmsSet")

# Step 1: Initialize annotation container
xa <- xsAnnotate(xset)

# Step 2: Group features by chromatographic FWHM
xa <- groupFWHM(xa, perfwhm = 0.6)

# Step 3: Group by peak shape correlation across samples
xa <- groupCorr(
  xa, 
  cor_eic_th = 0.75,     # EIC correlation threshold
  calcIso    = TRUE,     # Use isotope information in grouping
  calcCiS    = TRUE,     # Correlation inside samples
  calcCaS    = TRUE      # Correlation across samples
)

# Step 4: Identify isotope peaks (mass tolerance = 0.01 Da / 10 ppm)
xa <- findIsotopes(xa, maxcharge = 3, maxiso = 4, ppm = 10, mzabs = 0.01)

# Step 5: Annotate adducts in positive ionization mode
xa <- findAdducts(
  xa, 
  polarity   = "positive", 
  max_peaks  = 100, 
  rules      = defaultChecklist$rules_pos
)

# Step 6: Extract the deconvoluted peak list
peaklist_cam <- getPeaklist(xa)
```

The resulting `peaklist_cam` table contains three critical diagnostic columns:
- `pcgroup`: The pseudo-spectrum group ID. All features sharing a `pcgroup` originate from the same chemical entity.
- `isotopes`: Identifies monoisotopic peaks `[M]`, as well as `[M+1]`, `[M+2]` isotopic peaks.
- `adduct`: Specific assigned adduct formula (e.g., `[M+Na]+`, `[M+K]+`, `[M+H-H2O]+`).

::: {.callout-tip}
By filtering the feature table to retain only the primary monoisotopic features (discarding $[M+1]$ isotopes and redundant adducts for quantification), the dimensionality of the feature table is reduced by 60%–75%, dramatically mitigating the multiple-testing burden in Chapter 21.
:::

## Step 3: Tolerance-Aware Exact-Mass Database Searching

Because mass spectrometers measure with finite physical resolution, an observed mass must be matched against reference databases (HMDB, KEGG, ChEBI, LipidMaps) within an instrument-specific parts-per-million (ppm) window.

### Mathematical Formulation of Mass Accuracy

Mass error is expressed in absolute Daltons ($\Delta m$) or relative parts-per-million ($\text{ppm}$):

$$\Delta m = |m_{\text{obs}} - m_{\text{theor}}|$$

$$\text{ppm error} = \frac{|m_{\text{obs}} - m_{\text{theor}}|}{m_{\text{theor}}} \times 10^6$$

The allowable search window $[m_{\text{lower}}, m_{\text{upper}}]$ for a given tolerance $\tau_{\text{ppm}}$ is:

$$m_{\text{lower}} = m_{\text{obs}} \left( 1 - \frac{\tau_{\text{ppm}}}{10^6} \right), \quad m_{\text{upper}} = m_{\text{obs}} \left( 1 + \frac{\tau_{\text{ppm}}}{10^6} \right)$$

### Implementing Database Matching with `dplyr` Range Joins

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true
library(dplyr)

# 1. Reference database snippet
ref_db <- tibble::tribble(
  ~compound_name,  ~formula,       ~exact_mass,
  "Glucose",       "C6H12O6",      180.063388,
  "Fructose",      "C6H12O6",      180.063388,  # Stereoisomer
  "Inositol",      "C6H12O6",      180.063388,  # Stereoisomer
  "Caffeine",      "C8H10N4O2",    194.080376,
  "Citric acid",   "C6H8O7",       192.027003
)

# 2. Experimental observed monoisotopic masses from LC-MS run
query_features <- tibble::tribble(
  ~feature_id, ~observed_mass, ~retention_time,
  "FT_001",    180.0639,       2.45,
  "FT_002",    194.0801,       6.12,
  "FT_003",    312.1245,       9.80   # Unmatched unknown
)

# Match within 5 ppm tolerance

annotated_matches <- query_features %>%
  mutate(
    m_min = observed_mass * (1 - ppm_tol / 1e6),
    m_max = observed_mass * (1 + ppm_tol / 1e6)

  cross_join(ref_db) %>%
  filter(exact_mass >= m_min & exact_mass <= m_max) %>%
  mutate(
    ppm_error = round(abs(observed_mass - exact_mass) / exact_mass * 1e6, 2)

  select(feature_id, observed_mass, retention_time, compound_name, formula, ppm_error)
```

## Step 4: Isotopic Pattern Simulation and Envelope Scoring

Exact mass alone is often insufficient to specify a single chemical formula. Above $m/z = 300$, dozens of distinct elemental formulas fall within a 5 ppm window. 

To break formula degeneracy, high-resolution instruments measure the **isotopic fine structure**: the relative abundances and exact mass spacing of naturally occurring stable isotopes:
- Carbon: $^{12}\text{C}$ ($98.93\%$), $^{13}\text{C}$ ($1.07\%$)
- Nitrogen: $^{14}\text{N}$ ($99.63\%$), $^{15}\text{N}$ ($0.37\%$)
- Oxygen: $^{16}\text{O}$ ($99.76\%$), $^{18}\text{O}$ ($0.20\%$)
- Sulfur: $^{32}\text{S}$ ($94.99\%$), $^{34}\text{S}$ ($4.21\%$)
- Chlorine: $^{35}\text{Cl}$ ($75.76\%$), $^{37}\text{Cl}$ ($24.24\%$)

The theoretical abundance of the $M+1$ isotope for a molecule with $C$ carbon atoms is given by the binomial expansion:

$$\frac{I(M+1)}{I(M)} \approx C \times 1.07\% + N \times 0.37\%$$

A molecule containing sulfur or chlorine exhibits a prominent $M+2$ signature ($^{34}\text{S}$ at $+1.9958\text{ Da}$, $^{37}\text{Cl}$ at $+1.9970\text{ Da}$).

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true
# Inspect key isotope substitution mass shifts from MetaboCoreUtils
sub_rules <- MetaboCoreUtils::isotopicSubstitutionMatrix()
key_isotopes <- c("[13]C1", "[15]N1", "[18]O1", "[34]S1", "[37]Cl1")
unique_rules <- unique(sub_rules[sub_rules[, "name"] %in% key_isotopes, c("name", "md")])

data.frame(
  Isotope    = unique_rules[, "name"],
  Mass_Shift = round(unique_rules[, "md"], 6)
)

# Calculate theoretical isotopic envelope for Glucose (C6H12O6)
# M: Monoisotopic
# M+1: Driven by 6 x 13C (1.07%) + 12 x 2H (0.015%)
# M+2: Driven by 6 x 18O (0.205%) + C(6,2) x (13C)^2
c_count <- 6
h_count <- 12
o_count <- 6

p_m1 <- (c_count * 0.0107 + h_count * 0.00015) * 100
p_m2 <- (o_count * 0.00205 + choose(c_count, 2) * (0.0107^2)) * 100

data.frame(
  Peak           = c("M", "M+1 (13C)", "M+2 (18O / 13C2)"),
  Mass_Offset_Da = c(0.000000, 1.003355, 2.004246),
  Abundance_Pct  = c(100.0, round(p_m1, 2), round(p_m2, 2))
)
```

### Isotope Pattern Similarity Metric

The similarity between an experimentally observed isotopic envelope $\mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1, x_2)$ and a candidate theoretical distribution $\mathbf{y} = (y_0, y_1, y_2)$ is quantified using the normalized dot product (cosine correlation):

$$S_{\text{iso}} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^k x_i y_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^k x_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^k y_i^2}}$$

Candidate formulas with $S_{\text{iso}} < 0.90$ are rejected as chemically implausible, dramatically pruning false positive annotations.

## The Isomer Dilemma and Orthogonal Dimensions

In @sec-annotate-metabolites, `FT_001` matched three distinct compounds: Glucose, Fructose, and Inositol. All share the identical formula $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ and exact mass $180.063388\text{ Da}$. 

Mass spectrometry alone cannot resolve stereoisomers without orthogonal separation:

```
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                       Orthogonal Separation Dimensions                        |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. RETENTION TIME (RT) | Chromatographic interaction; requires local standard  |
| 2. ION MOBILITY (IMS)  | Collision Cross Section (CCS, A^2); gas-phase shape  |
| 3. TANDEM MS (MS/MS)   | Collision-induced dissociation; fragment fingerprints |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### Resolving Isomers:
1. **Retention Time Matching ($\Delta\text{RT}$)**: Authentic standards analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions allow discrimination between isomers. Glucose and galactose elute at distinct retention times on hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) columns.
2. **Ion Mobility Spectrometry (Collision Cross Section, CCS)**: Drift-tube and trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS) measure an ion's rotational collision cross section ($\text{CCS}$ in $\text{\AA}^2$). Structural isomers with different 3D shapes exhibit different drift times through a buffer gas.
3. **MS/MS Fragmentation**: As shown in Chapter 10, isomers frequently generate distinct diagnostic fragment ions or fragment intensity ratios under controlled collision energies.

## Synthesizing the Final Annotation Report

To prepare annotated data for statistical modeling, candidate matches must be folded into the `rowData` of a `SummarizedExperiment` object while preserving the rectangular integrity of the feature table:

```{r}
#| eval: true

# Collapse multiple candidate annotations into a delimited string
collapsed_report <- annotated_matches %>%
  group_by(feature_id, observed_mass, retention_time) %>%
  summarize(
    annotated_compounds = paste(compound_name, collapse = " | "),
    chemical_formulas   = paste(unique(formula), collapse = " | "),
    best_ppm_error      = min(ppm_error),
    match_count         = n(),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

# Join back with full feature table to classify unknowns
final_feature_metadata <- query_features %>%
  left_join(collapsed_report, by = c("feature_id", "observed_mass", "retention_time")) %>%
  mutate(
    annotated_compounds = ifelse(is.na(annotated_compounds), "Unknown", annotated_compounds),
    msi_confidence_level = case_when(
      annotated_compounds == "Unknown" ~ "Level 5 (Unknown)",
      match_count == 1 ~ "Level 3 (Putative Class / Match)",
      match_count > 1  ~ "Level 3 (Ambiguous Isomer Set)"
    )
  )

final_feature_metadata
```

## Common Pitfalls in Metabolite Annotation

| Pitfall | Consequence | Recommended Practice |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Assuming $[M+\text{H}]^+$ for all peaks** | If a feature is actually $[M+\text{Na}]^+$, the calculated neutral mass is incorrect by 21.98 Da, searching the database for an entirely wrong compound. | Always run `CAMERA` or `MetaboAnnotation` deconvolution to confirm adduct identity before database matching. |
| **Using wide mass tolerance ($\ge 30\text{ ppm}$)** | Produces hundreds of false positive database candidates per feature, rendering annotations useless. | Restrict searches to instrument-verified mass accuracy ($\le 5\text{ ppm}$ for Orbitrap/Q-TOF; calibrate externally). |
| **Reporting Level 1 without authentic standards** | Violates MSI standards; leads to irreproducible biological narratives in peer-reviewed manuscripts. | Restrict exact mass + MS/MS library hits to Level 2; reserve Level 1 for local authentic standard validation. |
| **Ignoring in-source fragmentation** | Degradation products (e.g., neutral water loss, decarboxylation) are misidentified as novel biological metabolites. | Check for co-eluting peaks with mass shifts corresponding to $-\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($-18.01056\text{ Da}$) or $-\text{CO}_2$ ($-43.98983\text{ Da}$). |
| **Neglecting stereoisomers** | Fails to recognize that exact mass cannot differentiate hexose stereoisomers (glucose, fructose, galactose). | Explicitly report ambiguous isomer sets in publications unless resolved by chromatography or authentic standards. |

---

## Summary

- Metabolite annotation transforms anonymous $m/z$ and retention time coordinates into biologically interpretable chemical structures.
- The **MSI confidence framework** governs reporting: Level 1 (authentic standard), Level 2 (spectral library match), Level 3 (compound class), Level 4 (molecular formula), and Level 5 (unknown).
- Electrospray ionization creates complex feature multiplet networks; **`CAMERA`** deconvolutes these into pseudo-spectra by correlating peak shapes ($r \ge 0.75$) and chromatographic retention times.
- **`MetaboCoreUtils`** provides rigorous mathematical tools for exact mass calculations, adduct interconversions, and theoretical isotopic distributions.
- Exact-mass database queries must control for relative parts-per-million (ppm) measurement error and should be validated using isotopic envelope scoring.
- Orthogonal analytical dimensions—retention time matching, ion mobility collision cross sections (CCS), and MS/MS fragmentation—are required to resolve isomeric ambiguity.

1. **Adduct Arithmetic**: Using `MetaboCoreUtils`, calculate the exact monoisotopic mass of the neurotransmitter Dopamine ($\text{C}_8\text{H}_{11}\text{NO}_2$). Compute the expected $m/z$ values for its $[M+\text{H}]^+$, $[M+\text{Na}]^+$, and $[M+\text{H}-\text{H}_2\text{O}]^+$ adducts in positive mode, and $[M-\text{H}]^-$ in negative mode.
2. **PPM Error Calculation**: An instrument measures a feature at $m/z = 341.1082$. A reference database lists Sucrose ($[M+\text{H}]^+$) at theoretical $m/z = 341.1078$. Calculate the absolute mass error in Daltons and the relative mass error in ppm. Does this match fall within a 5 ppm tolerance window?
3. **Isotopic Envelope Scoring**: Write an R script using `MetaboCoreUtils::isotopicDistribution()` to simulate the isotopic distributions of Palmitic Acid ($\text{C}_{16}\text{H}_{32}\text{O}_2$) and an isobaric sulfur-containing peptide fragment with identical nominal mass. How does the $M+2$ abundance differentiate the sulfur-containing compound from the lipid?
4. **CAMERA Pseudo-Spectrum Inspection**: Explain why two co-eluting features with correlation $r = 0.95$ and a mass difference of $\Delta m = 21.9819\text{ Da}$ are grouped into the same pseudo-spectrum. What are the chemical identities of these two peaks?
5. **MSI Level Audit**: A manuscript claims to have identified 450 metabolites in human serum using single-stage full-scan Orbitrap LC-MS matching against the HMDB exact mass database within 5 ppm. What MSI confidence level did the authors actually achieve, and how should their claims be revised?
