\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Quantitative mass spectrometry (MS) datasets are intrinsically sparse. In label-free proteomics (LFQ), data-dependent acquisition (DDA), and untargeted metabolomics, between 20\% and 50\% of the data matrix cells are typically unobserved. In clinical biomarker cohorts, this missingness presents an acute methodological dilemma: standard multivariate algorithms (such as Principal Component Analysis, Hierarchical Clustering, and Support Vector Machines) require complete matrices, yet the numerical substitution of missing values alters the variance structure, covariance relationships, and downstream statistical inferences of the entire dataset. 

Crucially, missingness in mass spectrometry does not arise from a single homogeneous process. A feature may be absent due to stochastic precursor selection during instrument duty cycles (Missing at Random; MAR), physical left-censoring below the instrument limit of detection (Missing Not at Random; MNAR), or true biological non-expression (e.g., tissue-specific proteins or gene knockouts). Applying a MAR algorithm (such as $k$-nearest neighbors) to an MNAR peptide invents artificial intermediate abundances for molecules that were genuinely absent, while applying left-censored substitution to MAR values introduces downward bias and artificially inflates differential abundance discoveries. This chapter establishes the rigorous statistical, biophysical, and computational foundations required to diagnose, model, and impute missing data in mass spectrometry. We formalize Rubin's missing data taxonomy within an analytical chemistry context, analyze the mathematical properties of deterministic, stochastic, and machine-learning imputation algorithms, derive Rubin's pooling rules for multiple imputation, and evaluate imputation-free probabilistic dropout modeling (proDA). The chapter concludes with a tutor diagnostic heuristics checklist, an end-to-end reproducible R laboratory, the ``Five Deadly Sins'' of omics imputation, and multi-tier exercises with exhaustive step-by-step worked solutions.
\end{abstract}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Tutor's Note: The Golden Rule of Group-Wise Filtering}\\
Never apply global missingness filters across an entire clinical cohort (e.g., ``remove all proteins with $>30\%$ missing values overall''). If a biomarker is abundant in Disease ($100\%$ detected in 10 patients) but silenced in Control ($0\%$ detected in 10 patients), global filtering eliminates this prime candidate. Always require $\ge 70\%$ detection \textit{within at least one condition} \cite{zhang2018dep, webb2020review}.
\end{notebox}

\section{Biophysical Taxonomies of Missingness in Mass Spectrometry}
% =============================================================================

In statistical theory, Donald Rubin~\cite{rubin1976inference} established the formal mathematical classification of missing data mechanisms based on the conditional distribution of the missingness indicator matrix.

\subsection{Rubin's Missing Data Taxonomy}

Let $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}$ denote the complete data matrix of $p$ molecular features across $n$ biological samples. We partition $\mathbf{Y} = (\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mis}})$, where $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}$ represents observed quantitative intensities and $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mis}}$ represents missing entries. Define the binary missingness response indicator matrix $\mathbf{M} \in \{0, 1\}^{p \times n}$ such that:
\begin{equation}
M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y_{ij} \text{ is missing} \\ 0 & \text{if } y_{ij} \text{ is observed} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The missing data mechanism is characterized by the conditional probability distribution $P(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{Y}, \boldsymbol{\phi})$, parameterized by $\boldsymbol{\phi}$:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Missing Completely at Random (MCAR):} Missingness is entirely independent of both observed and unobserved feature abundances:
    \begin{equation}
    P(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mis}}, \boldsymbol{\phi}) = P(\mathbf{M} \mid \boldsymbol{\phi})
    \end{equation}
    In MS instrumentation, MCAR occurs during random electrospray droplet instabilities, momentary liquid chromatography pressure spikes, or random ion guide transmission dropouts.
    
    \item \textbf{Missing at Random (MAR):} Missingness depends systematically on observed data, but is conditionally independent of the unobserved missing value itself:
    \begin{equation}
    P(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mis}}, \boldsymbol{\phi}) = P(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}, \boldsymbol{\phi})
    \end{equation}
    In DDA mass spectrometry, MAR arises from stochastic precursor selection: a peptide is present at moderate or high abundance, but competitive top-$N$ precursor scheduling selects a co-eluting isobaric ion, leaving the peptide unfragmented.
    
    \item \textbf{Missing Not at Random (MNAR):} Missingness depends directly on the unobserved value $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mis}}$:
    \begin{equation}
    P(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mis}}, \boldsymbol{\phi}) \ne P(\mathbf{M} \mid \mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}, \boldsymbol{\phi})
    \end{equation}
    This is the primary physical driver of missingness in analytical chemistry: analytes present below the instrument limit of detection (LOD) or limit of quantitation (LOQ) fail to generate a discernible chromatographic peak above baseline noise.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Instrumental Censoring vs. Biological Non-Expression}

A critical distinction must be drawn between analytical detection limits and biological absence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instrumental Left-Censoring (Analytical MNAR):} The protein or metabolite is physically expressed in the biological specimen, but its concentration falls below the ionization efficiency or detector sensitivity threshold ($y_{ij} < \text{LOD}$).
    \item \textbf{Biological Non-Expression (Structural Zero):} The gene is epigenetically silenced, unexpressed in the target tissue, or knocked out. The physical molecule count is identically zero ($y_{ij} \equiv 0$).
\end{itemize}

\begin{pitfallbox}
\textbf{The Tissue-Atlas Imputation Catastrophe:}\\
In multi-organ proteomics atlases (e.g., PXD010154 profiling 33,000 proteins across 12 human tissues), a liver-specific enzyme absent in cardiac muscle is missing due to biological regulation, not instrument failure. Imputing this enzyme in cardiac samples using left-censored algorithms introduces false molecular noise and destroys biological specificity. Never impute structural biological absence \cite{lazar2016accounting}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\subsection{Mathematical Diagnosis of Missingness Mechanisms}

To diagnose whether a dataset is dominated by MAR or MNAR, we fit a logistic regression model linking feature missingness probability to its mean observed abundance:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mnar_logistic}
\operatorname{logit}\left( P(M_{ij} = 1 \mid \bar{y}_i^{\text{obs}}) \right) = \ln\left( \frac{P(M_{ij} = 1)}{1 - P(M_{ij} = 1)} \right) = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \bar{y}_i^{\text{obs}}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{y}_i^{\text{obs}} = \frac{1}{|O_i|}\sum_{j \in O_i} y_{ij}$ denotes the mean observed $\log_2$ intensity for feature $i$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\gamma_1 \ll 0$ (strong negative slope; $p < 0.001$), missingness is heavily left-censored and abundance-dependent ($\implies$ \textbf{MNAR}).
    \item If $\gamma_1 \approx 0$ (flat slope across the dynamic range), missingness is abundance-independent ($\implies$ \textbf{MAR / MCAR}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Imputation-Free Modeling: Probabilistic Dropout Analysis (proDA)}
A modern paradigm shift championed by Ahlmann-Eltze and Huber \cite{ahlmann2020proda} avoids artificial value substitution altogether. The \texttt{proDA} algorithm formulates the probability of missingness via a smooth sigmoidal dropout curve:
\begin{equation}
P(M_{ij} = 1 \mid y_{ij}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left( \frac{y_{ij} - \rho_j}{\zeta_j} \right)}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_j$ is the 50\% inflection point (approximate LOD) and $\zeta_j$ governs the slope of the detection limit in sample $j$. By integrating this likelihood directly into the empirical Bayes linear model, \texttt{proDA} calculates posterior abundance and moderated $p$-values without corrupting variance structures through single imputation \cite{ahlmann2020proda}.

\subsection{Deterministic Minimum Value Imputation (MinDet)}

Deterministic substitution replaces all missing entries with the minimum observed value within the sample:
\begin{equation}
y_{ij}^* = \min_{k \in O_j}(y_{kj})
\end{equation}
While simple, MinDet introduces an artificial mathematical singularity: if 20\% of features receive identical values, the sample variance collapses, the empirical distribution exhibits a point-mass spike at $\min(y)$, and differential testing generates anti-conservative $p$-values.

\subsection{Left-Censored Stochastic Draws (Perseus \& QRILC)}

To preserve the continuous variance structure of analytical left-censoring, Tyanova et al.~\cite{tyanova2016perseus} formulated the left-shifted truncated Gaussian model. For sample column $j$ with observed mean $\hat{\mu}_j$ and standard deviation $\hat{\sigma}_j$, missing values are drawn stochastically:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:perseus_impute}
y_{ij}^* \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \hat{\mu}_j - \delta \hat{\sigma}_j, \; (\omega \hat{\sigma}_j)^2 \right)
\end{equation}
where canonical parameters are $\delta = 1.8$ standard deviations (downward shift) and $\omega = 0.3$ (distribution width shrinkage).

\subsection{Quantile Regression for Left-Censored Data (QRILC)}
Lazar et al.~\cite{lazar2016accounting} improved upon heuristic Perseus draws by estimating the parameters of a truncated Gaussian distribution via probit regression on observed empirical quantiles. QRILC reconstructs the unobserved left-hand tail of the distribution mathematically, preventing arbitrary user-parameter tuning.

\subsection{Multiple Imputation via Rubin's Pooling Rules}
Single imputation artificially treats imputed values as known truths, ignoring imputation uncertainty. Donald Rubin \cite{rubin1987multiple} established multiple imputation (MI):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Generate $M$ complete datasets ($M \approx 10$--$20$) through stochastic draws.
    \item Estimate the effect size $\hat{Q}_m$ and variance $\hat{U}_m$ in each dataset.
    \item Pool the results:
    \begin{align}
    \bar{Q} &= \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M \hat{Q}_m \\
    \bar{U} &= \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M \hat{U}_m \quad (\text{within-imputation variance}) \\
    B &= \frac{1}{M - 1}\sum_{m=1}^M (\hat{Q}_m - \bar{Q})^2 \quad (\text{between-imputation variance}) \\
    T &= \bar{U} + \left(1 + \frac{1}{M}\right) B \quad (\text{total pooled variance})
    \end{align}
    The test statistic $t = \bar{Q} / \sqrt{T}$ follows a $t$-distribution with degrees of freedom adjusted for missingness fraction \cite{vanbuuren2018, little2019statistical}.
\end{enumerate}

set.seed(42)

n_samples  <- 12 # 6 Control, 6 DrugB

group <- factor(rep(c("Control", "DrugB"), each = 6))
design <- model.matrix(~ 0 + group)
colnames(design) <- c("Control", "DrugB")
contrast_mat <- makeContrasts(DrugB_vs_Control = DrugB - Control, levels = design)

# Baseline log2 intensity ~ N(22, 2^2)
base_mat <- matrix(rnorm(n_proteins * n_samples, mean = 22, sd = 1.8), 
                   nrow = n_proteins, ncol = n_samples)
rownames(base_mat) <- paste0("PROT_", sprintf("%03d", 1:n_proteins))
colnames(base_mat) <- paste(group, rep(1:6, 2), sep = "_")

# Upregulate proteins 1-20 in DrugB (+2.5 log2FC)
# Downregulate proteins 21-40 in DrugB (-3.0 log2FC)
base_mat[1:20, 7:12]  <- base_mat[1:20, 7:12] + 2.5
base_mat[21:40, 7:12] <- base_mat[21:40, 7:12] - 3.0

# Introduce MNAR left-censoring below LOD = 19.5
raw_mat <- base_mat
raw_mat[raw_mat < 19.5] <- NA

cat(sprintf("Matrix dimensions: %d proteins x %d samples.\n", n_proteins, n_samples))
cat(sprintf("Total missingness rate: %.2f%%\n", sum(is.na(raw_mat)) / length(raw_mat) * 100))

# Generate M = 10 imputed datasets using Perseus stochastic draws
M <- 10
imputed_list <- vector("list", M)

for (m in 1:M) {
  mat_m <- raw_mat
  set.seed(m * 100)
  for (j in 1:ncol(mat_m)) {
    col_v  <- mat_m[, j]
    na_pos <- which(is.na(col_v))
    m_val  <- mean(col_v, na.rm = TRUE)
    s_val  <- sd(col_v, na.rm = TRUE)
    mat_m[na_pos, j] <- rnorm(length(na_pos), m_val - 1.8 * s_val, 0.3 * s_val)
  }
  imputed_list[[m]] <- mat_m
}

# Run limma across each of the M datasets
Q_matrix <- matrix(0, nrow = n_proteins, ncol = M)
U_matrix <- matrix(0, nrow = n_proteins, ncol = M)

for (m in 1:M) {
  fit <- lmFit(imputed_list[[m]], design)
  fit_c <- contrasts.fit(fit, contrast_mat)
  fit_b <- eBayes(fit_c)
  
  # logFC is Q; unmoderated variance of logFC is U
  Q_matrix[, m] <- fit_b$coefficients[, "DrugB_vs_Control"]
  U_matrix[, m] <- (fit_b$stdev.unscaled[, "DrugB_vs_Control"] * fit_b$s2.post)^2
}

# Apply Rubin's Pooling Formulas
Q_bar <- rowMeans(Q_matrix)
U_bar <- rowMeans(U_matrix)
B_var <- apply(Q_matrix, 1, var)
T_var <- U_bar + (1 + 1 / M) * B_var
SE_pooled <- sqrt(T_var)
t_pooled  <- Q_bar / SE_pooled
p_pooled  <- 2 * pt(-abs(t_pooled), df = 20)

rubin_df <- data.frame(
  Protein  = rownames(raw_mat),
  logFC    = round(Q_bar, 3),
  SE_pooled= round(SE_pooled, 3),
  t_stat   = round(t_pooled, 2),
  P_Value  = format.pval(p_pooled, digits = 3)
)

cat("--- Rubin's Multiple Imputation Pooled Results (Top 5 Features) ---\n")
print(head(rubin_df[order(p_pooled), ], 5))

\section{The Five Deadly Sins of Omics Imputation}
% =============================================================================

\begin{pitfallbox}
\textbf{Deadly Sin 1: Zero or Minimum Deterministic Imputation.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Replacing missing values with zero or the absolute minimum observed value ($y_{ij}^* = \min_k y$).\\
\textit{Consequence:} Spikes variance at zero, manufactures artificial multimodal distributions, and severely deflates standard errors, generating hundreds of false-positive discoveries \cite{lazar2016accounting}.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Use stochastic left-censored models (Perseus/QRILC) or multiple imputation \cite{tyanova2016perseus, rubin1987multiple}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}
\textbf{Deadly Sin 2: Imputing Before Normalization.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Imputing raw data matrices before sample loading or median normalization.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Sample-to-sample distance metrics reflect technical loading differences rather than true biological covariance, permanently distorting imputed values.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Always follow the order: Filter $\to$ Transform $\to$ Normalize $\to$ Impute.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}
\textbf{Deadly Sin 3: Applying MAR Algorithms ($k$-NN / missForest) to MNAR Features.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Imputing left-censored low-abundance features using donor-matching or tree-based regression.\\
\textit{Consequence:} The algorithm donor-matches with observed moderate-abundance features, assigning unphysiologically high values to peptides that were below LOD \cite{lazar2016accounting}.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Diagnose the missingness mechanism first; deploy QRILC or proDA for left-censored data \cite{ahlmann2020proda}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}
\textbf{Deadly Sin 4: Global Matrix Filtering Across Unbalanced Conditions.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Requiring $\ge 70\%$ presence across all samples combined.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Prime biomarkers that are present in Disease but completely absent in Control are systematically discarded.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Filter group-wise ($\ge 70\%$ in at least one biological group) \cite{webb2020review, zhang2018dep}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}
\textbf{Deadly Sin 5: Treating Single Imputation as True Known Measurements.}\\
\textit{Mechanism:} Imputing a single value and proceeding to standard hypothesis testing without accounting for imputation variance.\\
\textit{Consequence:} Single imputation fails to reflect estimation uncertainty, deflating confidence intervals and producing anti-conservative $p$-values.\\
\textit{Remedy:} Deploy Rubin's multiple imputation pooling rules or perform an imputation sensitivity audit \cite{rubin1987multiple, little2019statistical}.
\end{pitfallbox}

\subsection*{Solution to Exercise 19.1: Rubin's Variance Pooling}

\textbf{1. Pooled Point Estimate $\bar{Q}$:}\\
$$\bar{Q} = \frac{1}{5}(1.80 + 2.10 + 1.95 + 2.05 + 1.90) = \frac{9.80}{5} = 1.960$$

\textbf{2. Variance Components:}\\
$$\bar{U} = \frac{1}{5}(0.040 + 0.045 + 0.042 + 0.038 + 0.041) = \frac{0.206}{5} = 0.0412$$
The deviations from $\bar{Q} = 1.960$ are:
$$(-0.16, +0.14, -0.01, +0.09, -0.06)$$
The sample variance $B$ is:
\begin{align*}
B &= \frac{1}{4}\left((-0.16)^2 + (0.14)^2 + (-0.01)^2 + (0.09)^2 + (-0.06)^2\right) \\
  &= \frac{0.0256 + 0.0196 + 0.0001 + 0.0081 + 0.0036}{4} = \frac{0.0570}{4} = 0.01425
\end{align*}
The total variance $T$ incorporates the finite-imputation correction $(1 + 1/M) = 1.2$:
$$T = \bar{U} + \left(1 + \frac{1}{5}\right) B = 0.0412 + 1.2 \times 0.01425 = 0.0412 + 0.0171 = 0.0583$$

\textbf{3. Pooled Test Statistic and Interpretation:}\\
$$\text{SE}_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{T} = \sqrt{0.0583} \approx 0.2415$$
$$t = \frac{\bar{Q}}{\text{SE}_{\text{pooled}}} = \frac{1.960}{0.2415} \approx 8.118$$
Notice that if the analyst had naively used a single imputation (reporting $\text{SE} \approx \sqrt{\bar{U}} = \sqrt{0.0412} = 0.2030$), the apparent $t$-statistic would be $1.96 / 0.2030 = 9.656$. Rubin's rules correctly inflate the standard error by $19\%$ to account for missing value uncertainty, preventing anti-conservative false discoveries \cite{rubin1987multiple}.

\subsection*{Solution to Exercise 19.2: Perseus Imputation Tail Probability}

\textbf{1. Imputation Parameters:}\\
$$\mu_{\text{imp}} = \hat{\mu} - \delta \hat{\sigma} = 22.0 - 1.8(2.0) = 22.0 - 3.6 = 18.40$$
$$\sigma_{\text{imp}} = \omega \hat{\sigma} = 0.3(2.0) = 0.60$$
The imputed distribution is $\mathcal{N}(18.40, 0.60^2)$.

\textbf{2. Exceedance Probability:}\\
The physical LOD corresponds to the 15th percentile of the original $\mathcal{N}(22.0, 2.0^2)$ distribution:
$$z_{0.15} = \Phi^{-1}(0.15) \approx -1.0364 \implies \text{LOD} = 22.0 - 1.0364(2.0) \approx 19.927$$
We calculate the probability that an imputed value $Y_{\text{imp}} \sim \mathcal{N}(18.40, 0.60^2)$ exceeds $19.927$:
$$Z = \frac{19.927 - 18.40}{0.60} = \frac{1.527}{0.60} \approx 2.545$$
$$P(Y_{\text{imp}} > 19.927) = 1 - \Phi(2.545) \approx 1 - 0.9945 = 0.0055 \quad (0.55\%)$$
Only $0.55\%$ of imputed Perseus values exceed the detection threshold, confirming that the left-shift successfully restricts imputed features to the left-censored region below LOD \cite{tyanova2016perseus}.
