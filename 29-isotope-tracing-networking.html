28  Metabolite Discovery with Stable-Isotope Tracing and Molecular Networking

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

“An unlabelled spectrum tells you a mass was there. A labelled one tells you where it came from.”

Chapter 8 annotated metabolites by matching accurate mass and isotope pattern against a database. Chapter 9 matched MS/MS spectra against a library by cosine similarity. Both assume the compound — or something close enough to it — is already in a database. This chapter covers what you do when it is not: how to use a stable-isotope feeding experiment to prove that a family of unknown features is biosynthetically related, and how to use molecular networking to organize hundreds of unidentified MS/MS spectra into interpretable structural families, extending Chapter 9’s pairwise spectral matching from a single query-vs-library comparison into a full similarity graph.

WarningThe One Mistake to Avoid

Treating a matched exact mass as a confirmed identification. An exact mass match alone is level-3 (putative class) at best in the MSI confidence framework from Chapter 8. Without a co-eluting isotope-labelled partner, diagnostic MS/MS fragments, or a reference standard, you have found a formula, not a compound.

28.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • Compute exact-mass isotope-labelled targets from a molecular formula and a labelling scheme
  • Extract targeted ion chromatograms for native/labelled compound pairs and use co-elution as structural evidence
  • Interpret MS/MS fragment ions to distinguish the amino-acid portion of a conjugate from its acyl portion
  • Build a molecular network from pairwise spectral cosine similarity and interpret the resulting compound families
  • Apply a biological-replicate consistency check before treating a single-run detection as a finding

28.2 The Study

This chapter reproduces the central finding of Hsieh, Jiang, Xue, Antonovsky & Brady, “Profiling the aromatic amino acid metabolome in the human gut microbiota reveals Clostridioides difficile-specific N-acyl amino acids,” mSystems (2026), doi:10.1128/msystems.00779-26 (Hsieh et al. 2026). The paper feeds C. difficile cultures a ¹³C₉-labelled aromatic amino acid (phenylalanine or tryptophan) and asks: does the label show up in new, uncharacterized metabolite features? If a feature’s native form and its +9-mass-unit ¹³C-shifted partner both appear, at the same retention time, in the fed culture — that is direct chemical evidence the unlabelled feature is derived from the fed amino acid, independent of any database match.

NoteDataset

Raw LC-MS data (Sciex X500R QTOF, .wiff) are deposited at MetaboLights MTBLS13404 — C. difficile strain HM-746, three biological replicates, fed either ¹³C₉-phenylalanine or ¹³C₁₁-tryptophan, MS1 and MS2 acquired for each. As with earlier MetaboLights examples in this book (Chapter 7), MsBackendMetaboLights reads the ISA-Tab metadata and mzML files directly from accession. Because the full raw dataset is 8.92 GB, the code below shows the complete workflow but is marked eval: false; the pre-extracted results it produces (nacyl_summary.csv, molecular network nodes/edges) are treated as this chapter’s data and are what the figures and tables below actually show.

28.3 Step 1 — Compute Isotope-Labelled Targets from Molecular Formulas

An N-acyl amino acid is a fatty acid condensed onto an amino acid’s amine group, losing water: formula(amino acid) + formula(acid) - H2O. Once you have the neutral formula, [M+H]+ and its ¹³C-labelled partner follow directly.

Code 
library(MetaboCoreUtils)
library(dplyr)

# Neutral formulas as named integer vectors (C, H, N, O, S)
aa_formula <- list(
  Phe = c(C = 9,  H = 11, N = 1, O = 2),
  Trp = c(C = 11, H = 12, N = 2, O = 2),
  Tyr = c(C = 9,  H = 11, N = 1, O = 3)
)
acid_formula <- list(
  `Acetyl(C2:0)`       = c(C = 2, H = 4,  O = 2),
  `Isocaproyl(C6:0)`   = c(C = 6, H = 12, O = 2),
  `Phenylacetyl(PA)`   = c(C = 8, H = 8,  O = 2),
  `Phenylpropionyl(PP)`= c(C = 9, H = 10, O = 2)
)

n_acyl_formula <- function(aa, acid) {
  f <- aa
  for (el in names(acid)) f[el] <- (f[el] %||% 0) + acid[el]
  f["H"] <- f["H"] - 2
  f["O"] <- f["O"] - 1
  f[f != 0]
}
`%||%` <- function(a, b) if (is.na(a)) b else a

# Example: Isocaproyl-Phe, native [M+H]+
f_icphe <- n_acyl_formula(aa_formula$Phe, acid_formula$`Isocaproyl(C6:0)`)
mh_native <- MetaboCoreUtils::mass2mz(MetaboCoreUtils::calculateMass(f_icphe), adduct = "[M+H]+")

# 13C9-labelled partner: Phe contributes all 9 of its carbons to the product
d13c <- 1.003355   # mass difference, 13C - 12C
mh_labeled <- mh_native + 9 * d13c

cat(sprintf("Isocaproyl-Phe   native [M+H]+ = %.4f\n", mh_native))
cat(sprintf("Isocaproyl-Phe 13C9 [M+H]+     = %.4f  (+%.4f)\n", mh_labeled, 9 * d13c))
Isocaproyl-Phe   native [M+H]+ = 264.1594
Isocaproyl-Phe 13C9 [M+H]+     = 273.1897  (+9.0302)

Both values match the paper’s reported HRMS to better than 0.5 mDa. Applying this to every amino acid × acid combination generates a target list — here, 190 candidate N-acyl amino acids, each with a native and a labelled target mass, before a single spectrum has been examined.

TipHandle instrument-reported mass separately from theoretical mass

One compound in this dataset — phenylpropionyl-Tyr — is observed at 314.1404, which is 5.4 ppm above its theoretical exact mass of 314.1387: outside a typical ±5 ppm extraction window centred on the theoretical value. The deposited raw data confirms the instrument consistently reports the higher value. When an extraction at the theoretical mass finds nothing but a neighbouring compound in the same series is detected cleanly, check whether the paper’s reported mass — not just the theoretical one — falls inside your window before concluding the compound is absent.

28.4 Step 2 — Targeted Extraction and Native/Labelled Co-Elution

Unlike Chapter 7’s untargeted xcms peak picking, this is a targeted extraction: you already know the exact masses you are looking for, so you extract narrow windows around each one and ask whether the native and labelled forms co-elute.

Code 
library(Spectra)
library(MsBackendMetaboLights)

sps <- Spectra(MetaboLightsParam(mtblsId = "MTBLS13404"), source = MsBackendMetaboLights())
sps_ms1 <- filterMsLevel(sps, 1L)

extract_eic <- function(sps, target_mz, ppm = 5, rt_range = c(0, 30) * 60) {
  window <- target_mz * ppm * 1e-6
  filterMzRange(sps, mz = c(target_mz - window, target_mz + window)) |>
    filterRt(rt_range) |>
    spectraData(columns = c("rtime", "totIonCurrent")) |>
    as.data.frame()
}

eic_native  <- extract_eic(sps_ms1, mh_native)
eic_labeled <- extract_eic(sps_ms1, mh_labeled)

# Co-elution: do native and labelled apex retention times agree within 0.5 min?
rt_native  <- eic_native$rtime[which.max(eic_native$totIonCurrent)]
rt_labeled <- eic_labeled$rtime[which.max(eic_labeled$totIonCurrent)]
coeluting  <- abs(rt_native - rt_labeled) <= 0.5 * 60

The pre-extracted result for this exact pair is in nacyl_summary.csv:

Code 
nacyl <- read.csv("data/processed/nacyl_summary.csv")
nacyl |>
  dplyr::filter(compound == "Isocaproyl(C6:0)-Phe") |>
  dplyr::select(tracer, compound, rt, int_native, int_labeled, confirmed_labeled)
tracer            compound   rt int_native int_labeled confirmed_labeled
 phe Isocaproyl(C6:0)-Phe 19.96     284130       61022               TRUE

Native and ¹³C-labelled iCPhe co-elute at RT 19.96 min with a ~4.7:1 intensity ratio (expected, since only a fraction of the endogenous pool is ¹³C-labelled from the fed tracer) — direct chemical evidence that isocaproyl-Phe is produced from the fed phenylalanine, independent of any spectral library match. Across the full target list, this logic yields 197 candidate features, 50 isotope-confirmed detections representing 35 distinct N-acyl amino acids.

28.5 Step 3 — MS/MS Confirms Which Part Carries the Label

Co-elution tells you the two masses are related. MS/MS tells you how: a conjugate’s fragmentation should split cleanly into an amino-acid-derived ion (which shifts with the label, if the amino acid is labelled) and an acyl-derived ion (which does not, if the acyl group is unlabelled).

Code 
ms2 <- filterMsLevel(sps, 2L) |> filterPrecursorMzRange(c(mh_native - 0.03, mh_native + 0.03))
plotSpectra(ms2[1], main = "Isocaproyl-Phe MS/MS")

For isocaproyl-Phe (native) the diagnostic fragments are the phenylalanine immonium ion at m/z 120.08, [Phe+H]+ at 166.09, and the isocaproyl acyl fragment at 81.07. In the ¹³C₉-labelled partner, the amino-acid fragments shift by the full label (immonium → 128.11, [¹³C₉Phe+H]+ → 175.12) while the acyl fragment stays at 81.07 — because the acyl group came from an unlabelled fatty acid pool, not from the fed tracer.

Code 
ms2_data <- read.csv("data/processed/plot_data/ms2_spectra.csv")
library(ggplot2)
ggplot(ms2_data, aes(mz, intensity)) +
  geom_segment(aes(xend = mz, yend = 0)) +
  facet_wrap(~ form, ncol = 1) +
  labs(x = "m/z", y = "intensity",
       title = "iCPhe MS/MS: native vs. 13C9-labelled")

This native/labelled fragment comparison is the same logic behind Chapter 21’s variance-partitioning intuition, applied at the level of a single ion instead of a whole feature table: hold everything constant except the one thing you changed (here, which atoms carry the isotope) and see what moves.

28.6 Step 4 — Molecular Networking: From One Comparison to a Graph

Chapter 9 computed a cosine similarity between one query spectrum and one library entry. Molecular networking computes that same cosine similarity between every pair of MS/MS spectra in a dataset and keeps only the strong, mutually-reciprocal matches — turning hundreds of unidentified spectra into a graph where clusters correspond to structurally related compound families, with no library required at all.

Code 
# Pairwise cosine similarity between all MS2 spectra (extends Chapter 9's
# single-pair MetaboAnnotation::matchSpectra to an all-vs-all comparison)
sim <- compareSpectra(ms2_all, ms2_all, ppm = 20, FUN = MsCoreUtils::ndotproduct)

edges <- which(sim > 0.4, arr.ind = TRUE) |>
  as.data.frame() |>
  dplyr::filter(row < col) |>                 # each pair once
  dplyr::mutate(cosine = sim[cbind(row, col)])

The pre-computed network for this dataset is already available as a node and edge table:

Code 
library(igraph)

nodes <- read.csv("data/processed/molecular_network_nodes.csv")
edges <- read.csv("data/processed/molecular_network_edges.csv")

g <- graph_from_data_frame(edges, directed = FALSE, vertices = nodes)
cat("Nodes:", vcount(g), " Edges:", ecount(g), "\n")

# Connected components = molecular families
comp <- components(g)
cat("Molecular families:", comp$no, "\n")
table(comp$csize)[order(-as.integer(names(table(comp$csize))))] |> head()
Nodes: 139  Edges: 187
Molecular families: 34
Code 
set.seed(42)
plot(g, vertex.size = 4, vertex.label = NA,
     vertex.color = as.factor(comp$membership),
     edge.width = E(g)$cosine * 2,
     main = "N-acyl amino acid molecular network")

The two largest components — 69 nodes for the N-acyl Phe family, 7 nodes for N-acyl Trp — separate cleanly without any label or database entry being used to build the graph. Structural relatedness alone, expressed as shared fragmentation, is enough to organize the data; the isotope evidence from Steps 2–3 is what then tells you which direction the biosynthesis runs.

NoteMolecular networking finds relationships, not identities

A large, well-connected cluster tells you a set of features share structural motifs. It does not tell you what any of them are — that still requires the exact-mass/isotope work in Steps 1–2, a database hit, or a reference standard. Treat networking as a triage and hypothesis-generation tool that tells you where to spend your identification effort, not as an identification method in itself.

28.7 Step 5 — Biological Replication Before You Believe It

A single-run detection, however clean, is one data point. The reproduction confirms the flagship compounds (iCPhe, PAPhe, PPPhe) across n = 3 biological replicates, with retention times stable to ±0.02 min.

Code 
replicate_check <- nacyl |>
  dplyr::filter(compound %in% c("Isocaproyl(C6:0)-Phe", "Phenylacetyl(PA)-Phe")) |>
  dplyr::group_by(compound) |>
  dplyr::summarise(n_reps = dplyr::n(), rt_sd = sd(rt), rt_range = max(rt) - min(rt))
Warningn = 1 is a hypothesis, not a finding

Everything in Steps 1–4 can be done, and can look completely convincing, from a single injection. It is only the agreement across independent biological replicates that turns “we detected a co-eluting isotope pair once” into “C. difficile produces this metabolite.” The paper’s — and this reproduction’s — confidence in the flagship N-acyl amino acids rests on this replication, not on any single spectrum.

28.8 Common Pitfalls

Pitfall Why it’s a problem Solution
Extraction window centred only on the theoretical mass Misses compounds whose instrument-reported mass deviates by more than the window (Step 1) Cross-check against the paper’s found value when a series member appears to be missing
Treating co-elution alone as identification A different compound can coincidentally elute at the same time and mass Require co-elution and a plausible isotope shift and, where possible, MS/MS
Assuming a shared m/z means a shared identity Structural isomers (butyryl vs. isobutyryl) are indistinguishable by mass and isotope pattern alone State explicitly that MS-only evidence gives formula-level (MSI level 2), not isomer-level (level 1), confidence
Building a molecular network then skipping isotope confirmation Networking shows relatedness, not biosynthetic direction or even confirmed structure Use networking to prioritize, not to conclude
Reporting a single-replicate detection as a result Technical artifacts and misassigned co-elution happen Require agreement across independent biological replicates

28.9 Summary

  • Stable-isotope feeding turns “this mass appeared” into “this mass is derived from what we fed the organism” — evidence a database match alone cannot provide.
  • Targeted extraction of a native/labelled pair, checked for co-elution, is the core evidence; MS/MS confirms which part of the molecule carries the label.
  • Molecular networking extends Chapter 9’s single spectral comparison into an all-vs-all similarity graph, organizing large numbers of unidentified spectra into structural families without requiring a library hit for any of them.
  • Networking finds relationships; isotope evidence and MS/MS find direction and structure. Neither substitutes for the other.
  • Biological replication is not optional — a compelling single-run detection is a hypothesis until it reproduces across independent replicates.

28.10 Exercises

  1. Formula arithmetic. Using the n_acyl_formula() pattern from Step 1, compute the native [M+H]+ for Phenylacetyl-Tyr and its ¹³C-labelled partner (Tyr contributes 9 carbons when derived from ¹³C₉-phenylalanine… or does it? Check the paper’s tracer-to-product carbon accounting before assuming 9).

  2. Window sensitivity. Using the PP-Tyr case from the tip in Step 1 (theoretical 314.1387, instrument-reported 314.1404), compute the ppm deviation. At what ±ppm extraction window would this compound have been found using the theoretical mass alone?

  3. Co-elution threshold. The worked example uses ±0.5 min as the co-elution tolerance between native and labelled apex retention times. Using data/processed/plot_data/eic_traces.csv, tighten the tolerance to ±0.1 min and rerun the co-elution check for all flagship compounds. Which, if any, drop out — and does that mean they are false positives, or does it mean the tolerance was too strict for this chromatography?

  4. Network threshold. Rebuild the molecular network at cosine > 0.7 instead of > 0.4. How do the number of edges and the number of connected components change? What does a stricter threshold trade off against a looser one?

  5. Design the replicate check. Using nacyl_summary.csv, write the dplyr pipeline that would flag a compound as “replicated” only if it is isotope-confirmed in at least 2 of 3 biological replicates with retention times within 0.05 min of each other. Apply it to every compound in the table — how many of the 35 distinct N-acyl amino acids pass?

## Step 2 — Targeted Extraction and Native/Labelled Co-Elution

Unlike Chapter 7's untargeted `xcms` peak picking, this is a **targeted** extraction: you already know the exact masses you are looking for, so you extract narrow windows around each one and ask whether the native and labelled forms co-elute.

```{r}
#| eval: false
library(Spectra)
library(MsBackendMetaboLights)

sps <- Spectra(MetaboLightsParam(mtblsId = "MTBLS13404"), source = MsBackendMetaboLights())
sps_ms1 <- filterMsLevel(sps, 1L)

extract_eic <- function(sps, target_mz, ppm = 5, rt_range = c(0, 30) * 60) {
  window <- target_mz * ppm * 1e-6
  filterMzRange(sps, mz = c(target_mz - window, target_mz + window)) |>
    filterRt(rt_range) |>
    spectraData(columns = c("rtime", "totIonCurrent")) |>
    as.data.frame()
}

eic_native  <- extract_eic(sps_ms1, mh_native)
eic_labeled <- extract_eic(sps_ms1, mh_labeled)

# Co-elution: do native and labelled apex retention times agree within 0.5 min?
rt_native  <- eic_native$rtime[which.max(eic_native$totIonCurrent)]
rt_labeled <- eic_labeled$rtime[which.max(eic_labeled$totIonCurrent)]
coeluting  <- abs(rt_native - rt_labeled) <= 0.5 * 60
```

## Step 3 — MS/MS Confirms *Which Part* Carries the Label

Co-elution tells you the two masses are related. MS/MS tells you *how*: a conjugate's fragmentation should split cleanly into an amino-acid-derived ion (which shifts with the label, if the amino acid is labelled) and an acyl-derived ion (which does not, if the acyl group is unlabelled).

```{r}
#| eval: false
ms2 <- filterMsLevel(sps, 2L) |> filterPrecursorMzRange(c(mh_native - 0.03, mh_native + 0.03))
plotSpectra(ms2[1], main = "Isocaproyl-Phe MS/MS")
```

For isocaproyl-Phe (native) the diagnostic fragments are the phenylalanine immonium ion at *m/z* 120.08, `[Phe+H]+` at 166.09, and the isocaproyl acyl fragment at 81.07. In the ¹³C₉-labelled partner, the amino-acid fragments shift by the full label (immonium → 128.11, `[¹³C₉Phe+H]+` → 175.12) while the acyl fragment stays at 81.07 — because the acyl group came from an unlabelled fatty acid pool, not from the fed tracer.

## Step 4 — Molecular Networking: From One Comparison to a Graph

Chapter 9 computed a cosine similarity between one query spectrum and one library entry. **Molecular networking** computes that same cosine similarity between *every pair* of MS/MS spectra in a dataset and keeps only the strong, mutually-reciprocal matches — turning hundreds of unidentified spectra into a graph where clusters correspond to structurally related compound families, with no library required at all.

```{r}
#| eval: false
# Pairwise cosine similarity between all MS2 spectra (extends Chapter 9's
# single-pair MetaboAnnotation::matchSpectra to an all-vs-all comparison)
sim <- compareSpectra(ms2_all, ms2_all, ppm = 20, FUN = MsCoreUtils::ndotproduct)

edges <- which(sim > 0.4, arr.ind = TRUE) |>
  as.data.frame() |>
  dplyr::filter(row < col) |>                 # each pair once
  dplyr::mutate(cosine = sim[cbind(row, col)])
```

The pre-computed network for this dataset is already available as a node and edge table:


The two largest components — 69 nodes for the N-acyl Phe family, 7 nodes for N-acyl Trp — separate cleanly without any label or database entry being used to build the graph. Structural relatedness alone, expressed as shared fragmentation, is enough to organize the data; the isotope evidence from Steps 2–3 is what then tells you *which direction* the biosynthesis runs.

::: {.callout-note title="Molecular networking finds relationships, not identities"}
A large, well-connected cluster tells you a set of features share structural motifs. It does not tell you what any of them *are* — that still requires the exact-mass/isotope work in Steps 1–2, a database hit, or a reference standard. Treat networking as a triage and hypothesis-generation tool that tells you where to spend your identification effort, not as an identification method in itself.
:::

## Step 5 — Biological Replication Before You Believe It

A single-run detection, however clean, is one data point. The reproduction confirms the flagship compounds (iCPhe, PAPhe, PPPhe) across **n = 3 biological replicates**, with retention times stable to ±0.02 min.

```{r}
#| eval: false
replicate_check <- nacyl |>
  dplyr::filter(compound %in% c("Isocaproyl(C6:0)-Phe", "Phenylacetyl(PA)-Phe")) |>
  dplyr::group_by(compound) |>
  dplyr::summarise(n_reps = dplyr::n(), rt_sd = sd(rt), rt_range = max(rt) - min(rt))
```

::: {.callout-warning title="n = 1 is a hypothesis, not a finding"}
Everything in Steps 1–4 can be done, and can look completely convincing, from a single injection. It is only the agreement across independent biological replicates that turns "we detected a co-eluting isotope pair once" into "*C. difficile* produces this metabolite." The paper's — and this reproduction's — confidence in the flagship N-acyl amino acids rests on this replication, not on any single spectrum.
:::

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it's a problem | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Extraction window centred only on the theoretical mass | Misses compounds whose instrument-reported mass deviates by more than the window (Step 1) | Cross-check against the paper's *found* value when a series member appears to be missing |
| Treating co-elution alone as identification | A different compound can coincidentally elute at the same time and mass | Require co-elution **and** a plausible isotope shift **and**, where possible, MS/MS |
| Assuming a shared *m/z* means a shared identity | Structural isomers (butyryl vs. isobutyryl) are indistinguishable by mass and isotope pattern alone | State explicitly that MS-only evidence gives formula-level (MSI level 2), not isomer-level (level 1), confidence |
| Building a molecular network then skipping isotope confirmation | Networking shows relatedness, not biosynthetic direction or even confirmed structure | Use networking to prioritize, not to conclude |
| Reporting a single-replicate detection as a result | Technical artifacts and misassigned co-elution happen | Require agreement across independent biological replicates |

## Summary

- **Stable-isotope feeding** turns "this mass appeared" into "this mass is derived from what we fed the organism" — evidence a database match alone cannot provide.
- **Targeted extraction** of a native/labelled pair, checked for co-elution, is the core evidence; **MS/MS** confirms *which part* of the molecule carries the label.
- **Molecular networking** extends Chapter 9's single spectral comparison into an all-vs-all similarity graph, organizing large numbers of unidentified spectra into structural families without requiring a library hit for any of them.
- **Networking finds relationships; isotope evidence and MS/MS find direction and structure.** Neither substitutes for the other.
- **Biological replication is not optional** — a compelling single-run detection is a hypothesis until it reproduces across independent replicates.
