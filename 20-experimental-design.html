19  Experimental Design, Replication, and Power

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

No amount of sophisticated modelling rescues a confounded design. The most important statistics in a mass spectrometry study are decided before a single sample enters the instrument.

Every computational pipeline discussed in this book assumes that the acquired mass spectrometry data contains a recoverable biological signal. However, mass spectrometers are high-dimensional, drift-prone analytical instruments subject to matrix effects, ion suppression, column degradation, and mass calibration drift. Whether an experiment successfully answers its biological question depends primarily on its experimental design.

If an experiment is designed rigorously—with adequate biological replication, balanced blocking against nuisance factors, and randomized injection order—downstream statistical modeling with empirical Bayes shrinkage (limma) and linear mixed models is powerful and straightforward. Conversely, if technical covariates are confounded with biological conditions (e.g., all disease samples run on one day or on one multi-well plate, and all control samples on another), no statistical normalization, batch correction, or machine learning algorithm can separate biological truth from instrumental artifact.

This chapter bridges physical instrument acquisition (Parts I–IV) and downstream statistical inference (Parts V–VII). It provides the mathematical foundations, practical design architectures, sample size equations, and simulation tools required to design publication-grade proteomics and metabolomics experiments.

WarningThe One Mistake to Avoid

Confounding your condition with batch, plate, or run order. If every case is acquired on Monday and every control on Tuesday, the observed difference is an inseparable mixture of biology and day-to-day instrument drift. Randomize and block before acquiring a single sample.

19.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • Formulate MS experiments using classical design principles: replication, randomization, and local control (blocking)
  • Decompose total experimental variance into biological, sample preparation, injection, and residual error components
  • Distinguish biological units of replication from technical pseudoreplicates and model them appropriately
  • Formulate analytical power calculations and understand how multiple-testing corrections (FDR) reshape sample size requirements
  • Compute sample size inflations that account for feature missingness and left-censored detection limits
  • Build randomized complete block designs (RCBD) and balanced injection schedules with interleaved pooled QC standards
  • Implement Monte Carlo power simulations in R that mirror empirical Bayes moderation pipelines (limma)
  • Apply empirical sample size rules of thumb tailored to cell culture, animal models, and clinical human cohorts

19.2 Foundations of Experimental Design in Mass Spectrometry

Modern experimental design stems from Ronald A. Fisher’s foundational principles developed in agricultural statistics: replication, randomization, and local control (blocking). While Fisher worked with crop yields across physical field plots, liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (LC-MS) introduces analogous analytical “plots” subject to systematic environmental variation:

+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        Fisherian Design Triad for LC-MS                       |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. REPLICATION     | Captures biological variance; determines true power      |
| 2. RANDOMIZATION   | Breaks correlation between biology and instrument drift   |
| 3. LOCAL CONTROL   | Groups samples into homogeneous blocks (plates, batches) |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

19.2.1 1. Replication: Biological vs. Technical

In mass spectrometry, failure to distinguish between experimental units and observational units leads to pseudoreplication—the artificial inflation of statistical degrees of freedom:

  • Biological Replicates: Independent biological units (e.g., individual patients, separate mice, independently cultured cell passages). Biological replication captures genuine population variability (\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}). Statistical inference regarding biological populations depends strictly on biological replicate count (n_{\text{bio}}).
  • Technical Replicates: Repeated measurements of the same biological unit (e.g., duplicate tryptic digestions, repeated LC-MS injections from the same vial). Technical replicates capture measurement error (\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}). They increase measurement precision for that single unit, but they do not increase the effective sample size for biological testing.
Important

Averaging three technical injections of a single mouse yields an accurate measurement of that one mouse; it does not provide n = 3 for testing differences between genotypes. In limma, technical replicates must either be averaged prior to model fitting or modeled via duplicateCorrelation() with subject-level blocking (Chapter 21). Never treat technical replicates as independent columns in a design matrix.

19.2.2 2. Randomization

Mass spectrometers suffer from non-stationary behavior: - ESI source fouling causes progressive sensitivity loss over time - Chromatographic column aging alters retention times and peak widths - Ambient laboratory temperature fluctuations induce mass calibration drift (\Delta m/z)

If samples are injected in a naive order (e.g., all 20 Control samples followed by all 20 Treated samples), run order is perfectly collinear with treatment status. Any observed difference in peak area could be caused by biological induction or by a 15% drop in source transmission. Randomizing the acquisition sequence ensures that instrument drift appears as uncorrelated random noise rather than a spurious biological effect.

19.2.3 3. Local Control (Blocking)

When technical variation cannot be eliminated, it must be blocked. A block is a group of experimental units processed under relatively homogeneous conditions (e.g., a single 96-well digestion plate, an isobaric TMT 10-plex batch, or a single instrument calibration cycle).

By ensuring that every biological condition is represented equally within each block (balanced design), between-block variance can be estimated and subtracted from the residual error in linear models, substantially increasing statistical power.

19.3 Hierarchical Variance Decomposition

To understand why technical replication cannot rescue poor biological replication, consider the hierarchical linear model for measured intensity y_{ijk} of feature g in biological replicate j of condition i across technical injection k:

y_{ijk} = \mu + \alpha_i + b_{ij} + e_{ijk}

where: - \mu is the overall mean abundance - \alpha_i is the fixed biological effect of condition i (\sum \alpha_i = 0) - b_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{bio}}) is the random biological effect of subject j - e_{ijk} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{tech}}) is the random technical measurement error

The total variance of the mean across m technical replicates for a single biological subject is:

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}_{ij\cdot}) = \sigma^2_{\text{bio}} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}{m}

The variance of the estimated group treatment effect (\bar{y}_{i\cdot\cdot}) with n biological replicates per group is:

\operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}_{i\cdot\cdot}) = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{n} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}{n \cdot m}

19.3.1 The Law of Diminishing Returns in Technical Replication

The ratio of biological to technical variance determines experimental leverage. We define the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) as:

\text{ICC} = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}} + \sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}

In clinical proteomics and untargeted human metabolomics, inter-individual biological variation typically dominates analytical error (\sigma^2_{\text{bio}} \gg \sigma^2_{\text{tech}}, \text{ICC} > 0.80). Under these conditions:

\lim_{m \to \infty} \operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}_{i\cdot\cdot}) = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{n}

Increasing technical replicates (m) from 1 to 3 reduces only the small \sigma^2_{\text{tech}} term. In contrast, doubling biological replicates (n) halves both variance components. Unless technical error is exceptionally large (e.g., ultra-low input or single-cell proteomics; Chapter 26), resources should always be invested in biological replicates rather than repeated injections.

Scenario \sigma_{\text{bio}} \sigma_{\text{tech}} ICC Optimal Strategy
Human Plasma Metabolomics 0.90 0.20 0.95 Single injection; maximize clinical cohort size (n \ge 30)
Mouse Liver Proteomics 0.50 0.15 0.92 Single injection; 6–8 animals per group
HeLa Cell Perturbation 0.25 0.12 0.81 Single injection; 4–5 independent biological passages
Single-Cell MS (SCP) 0.30 0.60 0.20 Duplicate digestions/carriers; technical optimization

19.4 Statistical Power and Sample Size Determination

Statistical power (1 - \beta) is the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis (H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2) when a true biological difference of magnitude \delta exists.

Power depends on four interdependent variables: 1. Effect Size (\delta or \log_2\text{FC}): The minimal biologically meaningful difference between groups. 2. Variance (\sigma^2): The residual variance of feature abundances on the \log_2 scale. 3. Significance Level (\alpha): The per-feature type I error rate. 4. Sample Size (n): The number of independent biological replicates per group.

19.4.1 Analytical Formula for a Two-Group Comparison

For a single feature evaluated with a two-sided two-sample t-test with equal group sizes (n_1 = n_2 = n), the required sample size per group is given by:

n = \frac{2 \left( z_{1 - \alpha / 2} + z_{1 - \beta} \right)^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2} = \frac{2 \left( z_{1 - \alpha / 2} + z_{1 - \beta} \right)^2}{d^2}

where: - d = \frac{\delta}{\sigma} is Cohen’s standardized effect size - z_{1 - \alpha / 2} is the standard normal critical value for significance (e.g., 1.96 for \alpha = 0.05) - z_{1 - \beta} is the standard normal critical value for power (e.g., 0.84 for 80% power, 1.28 for 90% power)

Code 
# Analytical calculation using the 'pwr' package
library(pwr)

log2fc     <- 1.0     # 2-fold change
sigma      <- 0.7     # log2 standard deviation (from pilot data)
d_effect   <- log2fc / sigma

# Single-test calculation at nominal alpha = 0.05
pwr::pwr.t.test(d = d_effect, sig.level = 0.05, power = 0.80, type = "two.sample")

19.4.2 High-Throughput Multiplicity Adjustment

In an untargeted proteomics or metabolomics experiment, testing is performed across M features simultaneously (M = 3,000 to 10,000). Evaluating thousands of hypotheses at nominal \alpha = 0.05 produces hundreds of false positives.

To control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) at target level q (typically 0.05), the per-test significance threshold \alpha^* must be substantially more stringent.

Using the conservative Bonferroni bound across M features:

\alpha^* \approx \frac{q}{M}

For M = 5,000 features and target \text{FDR} = 0.05, the effective per-feature significance threshold drops to:

\alpha^* = \frac{0.05}{5000} = 1 \times 10^{-5}

This shifts the critical normal quantile z_{1 - \alpha / 2} from 1.96 to approximately 4.42, dramatically increasing the required sample size:

Code 
# Sample size adjusted for 5,000 multiple comparisons
m_features   <- 5000
alpha_adj    <- 0.05 / m_features

pwr::pwr.t.test(d = d_effect, sig.level = alpha_adj, power = 0.80, type = "two.sample")

Under this conservative Bonferroni correction, detecting a 2-fold change (\log_2\text{FC} = 1.0) with \sigma = 0.7 requires n \approx 18 biological replicates per group, compared to n \approx 8 for a single hypothesis.

19.4.3 Sample Size Inflation for Missing Data

Mass spectrometry data exhibits non-negligible missingness (Chapter 18), especially for low-abundance analytes near the limit of detection (LOD).

If a feature suffers from missingness proportion p_{\text{miss}}, the effective sample size is reduced to:

n_{\text{eff}} = n \cdot (1 - p_{\text{miss}})

To guarantee that a study retains nominal statistical power (n_{\text{req}}) even for features subject to typical experimental dropout, the planned sample size n^* must be inflated:

n^* = \left\lceil \frac{n_{\text{req}}}{1 - p_{\text{miss}}} \right\rceil

For example, if an analytical power calculation demands n_{\text{req}} = 8 complete observations per group, and the expected peptide dropout rate in complex plasma digests is p_{\text{miss}} = 0.25, the enrolled sample size must be:

n^* = \left\lceil \frac{8}{1 - 0.25} \right\rceil = 11 \text{ subjects per group}

19.5 Practical Design Architectures

19.5.1 1. Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD)

When samples must be split across multi-well plates, digestion days, or instrument runs, condition assignment must be blocked. In an RCBD, each block contains an equal proportion of each biological group:

Plate 1 (Block 1):   [Ctrl: 24]   [Case: 24]   [Pool QC: 4]
Plate 2 (Block 2):   [Ctrl: 24]   [Case: 24]   [Pool QC: 4]

In the linear model, plate is included as an additive blocking covariate:

y_{ij} = \mu + \text{Condition}_i + \text{Plate}_j + \epsilon_{ij}

This isolates between-plate variance from the residual error without confounding treatment effects.

19.5.2 2. Run-Order Randomization and Quality Control Interleaving

Within each analytical block, the injection order must be randomized, and pooled quality control (QC) samples must be interleaved at fixed intervals:

Code 
# Generate a randomized injection sequence with interleaved QC samples
set.seed(42)

# Biological cohort metadata: 30 cases, 30 controls
samples <- data.frame(
  sample_id = paste0("SMP_", sprintf("%03d", 1:60)),
  condition = rep(c("Control", "Disease"), each = 30)
)

# Randomize injection order of biological samples
rand_order <- samples[sample(nrow(samples)), ]

# Build sequence with 5 conditioning QCs at start and 1 QC every 8 injections
sequence <- list()
qc_count <- 1

# Pre-run column conditioning injections
for (k in 1:5) {
  sequence[[length(sequence) + 1]] <- data.frame(
    injection_order = length(sequence) + 1,
    sample_id       = paste0("QC_COND_", k),
    sample_type     = "QC_Conditioning",
    condition       = "QC"
  )
}

# Interleave biological samples and periodic QCs
for (i in seq_len(nrow(rand_order))) {
  sequence[[length(sequence) + 1]] <- data.frame(
    injection_order = length(sequence) + 1,
    sample_id       = rand_order$sample_id[i],
    sample_type     = "Biological",
    condition       = rand_order$condition[i]
  )
  
  if (i %% 8 == 0) {
    sequence[[length(sequence) + 1]] <- data.frame(
      injection_order = length(sequence) + 1,
      sample_id       = paste0("QC_POOL_", sprintf("%02d", qc_count)),
      sample_type     = "QC_Pool",
      condition       = "QC"
    )
    qc_count <- qc_count + 1
  }
}

injection_schedule <- do.call(rbind, sequence)
head(injection_schedule, 12)

19.5.3 3. The Confounding Diagnostic Check

Before acquiring the first sample, verify that experimental conditions are uncorrelated with technical variables:

Code 
# 1. Contingency table check across batches (want balanced cell frequencies)
table(injection_schedule$condition, injection_schedule$batch)

# 2. Pearson correlation with run order (must be approximately 0)
bio_only <- injection_schedule[injection_schedule$sample_type == "Biological", ]
cor_val  <- cor(as.numeric(factor(bio_only$condition)), bio_only$injection_order)
cat(sprintf("Correlation between Condition and Run Order: r = %.4f\n", cor_val))
stopifnot(abs(cor_val) < 0.10)

19.6 Simulation-Based Power Analysis

Standard analytical formulas assume normal, homoscedastic errors and ignore empirical Bayes variance moderation (limma). In modern proteomics and metabolomics, limma shrinks individual feature variances toward a pooled prior distribution, boosting statistical power for small sample sizes.

A Monte Carlo simulation provides the most accurate power estimation because it subjects simulated data to the exact computational pipeline used for the real study:

Code 
library(limma)

simulate_ms_power <- function(n_per_group, 
                              n_features = 2000, 
                              n_de       = 100, 
                              log2fc     = 1.0, 
                              sigma_mean = 0.6, 
                              n_sim      = 50) {
  power_estimates <- numeric(n_sim)
  
  for (s in seq_len(n_sim)) {
    # Generate design matrix
    group  <- factor(rep(c("Control", "Treated"), each = n_per_group))
    design <- model.matrix(~ group)
    
    # Simulate feature standard deviations from an inverse gamma distribution
    # mimicking empirical Bayes prior distributions
    prior_df   <- 4
    feature_sd <- sqrt(sigma_mean^2 * prior_df / rchisq(n_features, df = prior_df))
    
    # Generate null feature matrix
    mat <- matrix(rnorm(n_features * 2 * n_per_group, 
                        mean = 0, 
                        sd = rep(feature_sd, 2 * n_per_group)), 
                  nrow = n_features, byrow = FALSE)
    
    # Inject biological true differences into first n_de features
    mat[1:n_de, group == "Treated"] <- mat[1:n_de, group == "Treated"] + log2fc
    
    # Fit empirical Bayes moderated linear model
    fit  <- eBayes(lmFit(mat, design))
    padj <- p.adjust(fit$p.value[, 2], method = "BH")
    
    # Calculate recovery rate of true positive features at 5% FDR
    power_estimates[s] <- mean(padj[1:n_de] < 0.05)
  }
  
  mean(power_estimates)
}

# Test across small cohort candidate sizes
sample_sizes <- c(3, 5, 8, 12)
sim_results  <- sapply(sample_sizes, simulate_ms_power, log2fc = 1.0, n_sim = 20)

data.frame(
  Replicates_Per_Group = sample_sizes,
  Estimated_Power      = round(sim_results, 3)
)
  Replicates_Per_Group Estimated_Power
1                    3           0.000
2                    5           0.007
3                    8           0.251
4                   12           0.585

19.6.2 Key Takeaways from Figure 19.1:

  1. Large Effect Sizes (\Delta = 2.0, 4-fold change): Reach 80% power with n \approx 4 biological replicates per group.
  2. Moderate Effect Sizes (\Delta = 1.0, 2-fold change): Require n \ge 8 biological replicates per group to clear the 80% threshold under Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control.
  3. Subtle Effect Sizes (\Delta = 0.5, 1.4-fold change): Typical of complex polygenic phenotypes, drug dose-responses, or clinical plasma biomarkers. These require n \ge 20 replicates per group; studies with n = 3 to 5 have less than 20% power to detect these effects and will capture only extreme false-positive outliers.

19.7 Field-Specific Sample Size Guidelines

Based on typical variance parameters observed across thousands of published Bioconductor studies, we recommend the following minimum biological replication tiers:

Experimental System Expected Biological SD (\log_2) Recommended n per Group Justification & Caveats
Immortalized Cell Lines 0.20 – 0.40 4 – 5 Genetically identical; controlled media. Must use independent passages/seedings, not split dishes.
Primary Cell Cultures 0.40 – 0.70 6 – 8 Donor-to-donor variability introduces substantial biological heterogeneity.
Inbred Animal Models (e.g., C57BL/6) 0.40 – 0.60 6 – 10 Minimal genetic variance, but organ harvesting and feeding states introduce physiological drift.
Outbred Animals / Wild-Type 0.70 – 1.00 12 – 15 Genetic diversity inflates residual variance.
Human Biofluids (Plasma, Serum, Urine) 0.80 – 1.40 30 – 100+ Extreme demographic, dietary, and diurnal variance. Low-abundance markers suffer from high CVs.
Human Biopsies (Tumor vs. Normal) 0.90 – 1.50 40 – 120+ Tumor cellularity, necrosis, and stromal infiltration require large cohorts for robust signature discovery.

Before preparing reagents or submitting vials to the mass spectrometry facility, complete this verification checklist:

19.9 Summary

  • The experimental design decided before acquisition sets an immutable ceiling on the analytical and biological validity of your study.
  • Biological replicates determine statistical power to generalize findings; technical replicates quantify instrument precision and should not be pseudoreplicated.
  • Randomization protects against time-dependent instrument drift; blocking neutralizes unavoidable batch effects (plates, days, TMT plexes).
  • Multiple hypothesis testing across thousands of features shifts the required per-test significance threshold by orders of magnitude (\alpha^* \ll 0.05), necessitating larger cohorts than classical single-gene assays.
  • Incorporating empirical Bayes moderation (limma) via Monte Carlo simulation provides an accurate, realistic assessment of statistical power before committing laboratory resources.

19.10 Exercises

  1. Confounding Audit: A collaborator brings you a pilot study of 20 cancer patients and 20 healthy controls. In the metadata, all 20 cancer samples were extracted on Monday using Reagent Lot A and run on Column 1. All 20 controls were extracted on Tuesday using Reagent Lot B on Column 2. Explain formally why no statistical method (limma, ComBat, or mixed models) can identify true cancer biomarkers from this dataset. Propose an optimal 2-day re-extraction and acquisition schedule.
  2. Replicate Modeling: You have 5 biological subjects per group, and each subject was injected into the LC-MS in triplicate (N = 30 total runs). Write the R code to construct a limma design matrix and run duplicateCorrelation() to model the technical replicates without committing pseudoreplication.
  3. Power under Multiplicity: Using pwr::pwr.t.test(), compute the sample size needed to detect a 1.5-fold change (\log_2\text{FC} = 0.585) with residual standard deviation \sigma = 0.65 at 80% power under:
    • A single-hypothesis test (\alpha = 0.05)
    • An untargeted experiment testing 8,000 features with Bonferroni FDR adjustment (\alpha = 0.05 / 8000)
  4. Missingness Impact: A pilot metabolomics experiment indicates that candidate biomarker features suffer from approximately 25% missing values (p_{\text{miss}} = 0.25). If your power analysis specifies that n = 12 observations per group are needed, what is the minimum number of patients you must recruit per group?
  5. Isobaric Multiplex Layout: You have 4 biological conditions (A, B, C, D) with 6 biological replicates each (24 total samples). You have access to TMT 10-plex reagents. Design a balanced 3-plex layout that distributes conditions evenly and reserves Channel 126 in every plex for a common pooled reference standard.

## Foundations of Experimental Design in Mass Spectrometry

Modern experimental design stems from Ronald A. Fisher's foundational principles developed in agricultural statistics: **replication**, **randomization**, and **local control (blocking)**. While Fisher worked with crop yields across physical field plots, liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (LC-MS) introduces analogous analytical "plots" subject to systematic environmental variation:

```
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        Fisherian Design Triad for LC-MS                       |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. REPLICATION     | Captures biological variance; determines true power      |
| 2. RANDOMIZATION   | Breaks correlation between biology and instrument drift   |
| 3. LOCAL CONTROL   | Groups samples into homogeneous blocks (plates, batches) |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 1. Replication: Biological vs. Technical

In mass spectrometry, failure to distinguish between experimental units and observational units leads to **pseudoreplication**—the artificial inflation of statistical degrees of freedom:

- **Biological Replicates**: Independent biological units (e.g., individual patients, separate mice, independently cultured cell passages). Biological replication captures genuine population variability ($\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}$). Statistical inference regarding biological populations depends *strictly* on biological replicate count ($n_{\text{bio}}$).
- **Technical Replicates**: Repeated measurements of the *same* biological unit (e.g., duplicate tryptic digestions, repeated LC-MS injections from the same vial). Technical replicates capture measurement error ($\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}$). They increase measurement precision for that single unit, but they **do not** increase the effective sample size for biological testing.

::: {.callout-important}
Averaging three technical injections of a single mouse yields an accurate measurement of *that one mouse*; it does not provide $n = 3$ for testing differences between genotypes. In `limma`, technical replicates must either be averaged prior to model fitting or modeled via `duplicateCorrelation()` with subject-level blocking (Chapter 21). Never treat technical replicates as independent columns in a design matrix.
:::

### 2. Randomization

Mass spectrometers suffer from non-stationary behavior:
- ESI source fouling causes progressive sensitivity loss over time
- Chromatographic column aging alters retention times and peak widths
- Ambient laboratory temperature fluctuations induce mass calibration drift ($\Delta m/z$)

If samples are injected in a naive order (e.g., all 20 Control samples followed by all 20 Treated samples), run order is perfectly collinear with treatment status. Any observed difference in peak area could be caused by biological induction or by a 15% drop in source transmission. Randomizing the acquisition sequence ensures that instrument drift appears as uncorrelated random noise rather than a spurious biological effect.

### 3. Local Control (Blocking)

When technical variation cannot be eliminated, it must be **blocked**. A block is a group of experimental units processed under relatively homogeneous conditions (e.g., a single 96-well digestion plate, an isobaric TMT 10-plex batch, or a single instrument calibration cycle).

By ensuring that every biological condition is represented equally within each block (balanced design), between-block variance can be estimated and subtracted from the residual error in linear models, substantially increasing statistical power.

## Hierarchical Variance Decomposition

To understand why technical replication cannot rescue poor biological replication, consider the hierarchical linear model for measured intensity $y_{ijk}$ of feature $g$ in biological replicate $j$ of condition $i$ across technical injection $k$:

$$y_{ijk} = \mu + \alpha_i + b_{ij} + e_{ijk}$$

where:
- $\mu$ is the overall mean abundance
- $\alpha_i$ is the fixed biological effect of condition $i$ ($\sum \alpha_i = 0$)
- $b_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{bio}})$ is the random biological effect of subject $j$
- $e_{ijk} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{tech}})$ is the random technical measurement error

The total variance of the mean across $m$ technical replicates for a single biological subject is:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}_{ij\cdot}) = \sigma^2_{\text{bio}} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}{m}$$

The variance of the estimated group treatment effect ($\bar{y}_{i\cdot\cdot}$) with $n$ biological replicates per group is:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}_{i\cdot\cdot}) = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{n} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}{n \cdot m}$$

### The Law of Diminishing Returns in Technical Replication

The ratio of biological to technical variance determines experimental leverage. We define the **Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)** as:

$$\text{ICC} = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}} + \sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}$$

In clinical proteomics and untargeted human metabolomics, inter-individual biological variation typically dominates analytical error ($\sigma^2_{\text{bio}} \gg \sigma^2_{\text{tech}}$, $\text{ICC} > 0.80$). Under these conditions:

$$\lim_{m \to \infty} \operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}_{i\cdot\cdot}) = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{n}$$

Increasing technical replicates ($m$) from 1 to 3 reduces only the small $\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}$ term. In contrast, doubling biological replicates ($n$) halves both variance components. Unless technical error is exceptionally large (e.g., ultra-low input or single-cell proteomics; Chapter 26), **resources should always be invested in biological replicates rather than repeated injections**.

| Scenario | $\sigma_{\text{bio}}$ | $\sigma_{\text{tech}}$ | ICC | Optimal Strategy |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---|
| **Human Plasma Metabolomics** | 0.90 | 0.20 | 0.95 | Single injection; maximize clinical cohort size ($n \ge 30$) |
| **Mouse Liver Proteomics** | 0.50 | 0.15 | 0.92 | Single injection; 6–8 animals per group |
| **HeLa Cell Perturbation** | 0.25 | 0.12 | 0.81 | Single injection; 4–5 independent biological passages |
| **Single-Cell MS (SCP)** | 0.30 | 0.60 | 0.20 | Duplicate digestions/carriers; technical optimization |

## Statistical Power and Sample Size Determination

**Statistical power** ($1 - \beta$) is the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis ($H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$) when a true biological difference of magnitude $\delta$ exists.

Power depends on four interdependent variables:
1. **Effect Size ($\delta$ or $\log_2\text{FC}$)**: The minimal biologically meaningful difference between groups.
2. **Variance ($\sigma^2$)**: The residual variance of feature abundances on the $\log_2$ scale.
3. **Significance Level ($\alpha$)**: The per-feature type I error rate.
4. **Sample Size ($n$)**: The number of independent biological replicates per group.

### Analytical Formula for a Two-Group Comparison

For a single feature evaluated with a two-sided two-sample $t$-test with equal group sizes ($n_1 = n_2 = n$), the required sample size per group is given by:

$$n = \frac{2 \left( z_{1 - \alpha / 2} + z_{1 - \beta} \right)^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2} = \frac{2 \left( z_{1 - \alpha / 2} + z_{1 - \beta} \right)^2}{d^2}$$

where:
- $d = \frac{\delta}{\sigma}$ is Cohen's standardized effect size
- $z_{1 - \alpha / 2}$ is the standard normal critical value for significance (e.g., 1.96 for $\alpha = 0.05$)
- $z_{1 - \beta}$ is the standard normal critical value for power (e.g., 0.84 for 80% power, 1.28 for 90% power)

```{r}
#| eval: false
# Analytical calculation using the 'pwr' package
library(pwr)

log2fc     <- 1.0     # 2-fold change
sigma      <- 0.7     # log2 standard deviation (from pilot data)
d_effect   <- log2fc / sigma

# Single-test calculation at nominal alpha = 0.05
pwr::pwr.t.test(d = d_effect, sig.level = 0.05, power = 0.80, type = "two.sample")
```

### High-Throughput Multiplicity Adjustment

In an untargeted proteomics or metabolomics experiment, testing is performed across $M$ features simultaneously ($M = 3,000$ to $10,000$). Evaluating thousands of hypotheses at nominal $\alpha = 0.05$ produces hundreds of false positives.

To control the **False Discovery Rate (FDR)** at target level $q$ (typically $0.05$), the per-test significance threshold $\alpha^*$ must be substantially more stringent. 

Using the conservative Bonferroni bound across $M$ features:

$$\alpha^* \approx \frac{q}{M}$$

For $M = 5,000$ features and target $\text{FDR} = 0.05$, the effective per-feature significance threshold drops to:

$$\alpha^* = \frac{0.05}{5000} = 1 \times 10^{-5}$$

This shifts the critical normal quantile $z_{1 - \alpha / 2}$ from $1.96$ to approximately $4.42$, dramatically increasing the required sample size:

```{r}
#| eval: false
# Sample size adjusted for 5,000 multiple comparisons
m_features   <- 5000
alpha_adj    <- 0.05 / m_features

pwr::pwr.t.test(d = d_effect, sig.level = alpha_adj, power = 0.80, type = "two.sample")
```

Under this conservative Bonferroni correction, detecting a 2-fold change ($\log_2\text{FC} = 1.0$) with $\sigma = 0.7$ requires $n \approx 18$ biological replicates per group, compared to $n \approx 8$ for a single hypothesis.

### Sample Size Inflation for Missing Data

Mass spectrometry data exhibits non-negligible missingness (Chapter 18), especially for low-abundance analytes near the limit of detection (LOD). 

If a feature suffers from missingness proportion $p_{\text{miss}}$, the effective sample size is reduced to:

$$n_{\text{eff}} = n \cdot (1 - p_{\text{miss}})$$

To guarantee that a study retains nominal statistical power ($n_{\text{req}}$) even for features subject to typical experimental dropout, the planned sample size $n^*$ must be inflated:

$$n^* = \left\lceil \frac{n_{\text{req}}}{1 - p_{\text{miss}}} \right\rceil$$

For example, if an analytical power calculation demands $n_{\text{req}} = 8$ complete observations per group, and the expected peptide dropout rate in complex plasma digests is $p_{\text{miss}} = 0.25$, the enrolled sample size must be:

$$n^* = \left\lceil \frac{8}{1 - 0.25} \right\rceil = 11 \text{ subjects per group}$$

## Practical Design Architectures

### 1. Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD)

When samples must be split across multi-well plates, digestion days, or instrument runs, condition assignment must be blocked. In an RCBD, each block contains an equal proportion of each biological group:

```
Plate 1 (Block 1):   [Ctrl: 24]   [Case: 24]   [Pool QC: 4]
Plate 2 (Block 2):   [Ctrl: 24]   [Case: 24]   [Pool QC: 4]
```

In the linear model, plate is included as an additive blocking covariate:

$$y_{ij} = \mu + \text{Condition}_i + \text{Plate}_j + \epsilon_{ij}$$

This isolates between-plate variance from the residual error without confounding treatment effects.

### 2. Run-Order Randomization and Quality Control Interleaving

Within each analytical block, the injection order must be randomized, and pooled quality control (QC) samples must be interleaved at fixed intervals:

```{r}
#| eval: false
# Generate a randomized injection sequence with interleaved QC samples
set.seed(42)

# Biological cohort metadata: 30 cases, 30 controls
samples <- data.frame(
  sample_id = paste0("SMP_", sprintf("%03d", 1:60)),
  condition = rep(c("Control", "Disease"), each = 30)
)

# Randomize injection order of biological samples
rand_order <- samples[sample(nrow(samples)), ]

# Build sequence with 5 conditioning QCs at start and 1 QC every 8 injections
sequence <- list()
qc_count <- 1

# Pre-run column conditioning injections
for (k in 1:5) {
  sequence[[length(sequence) + 1]] <- data.frame(
    injection_order = length(sequence) + 1,
    sample_id       = paste0("QC_COND_", k),
    sample_type     = "QC_Conditioning",
    condition       = "QC"
  )
}

# Interleave biological samples and periodic QCs
for (i in seq_len(nrow(rand_order))) {
  sequence[[length(sequence) + 1]] <- data.frame(
    injection_order = length(sequence) + 1,
    sample_id       = rand_order$sample_id[i],
    sample_type     = "Biological",
    condition       = rand_order$condition[i]
  )
  
  if (i %% 8 == 0) {
    sequence[[length(sequence) + 1]] <- data.frame(
      injection_order = length(sequence) + 1,
      sample_id       = paste0("QC_POOL_", sprintf("%02d", qc_count)),
      sample_type     = "QC_Pool",
      condition       = "QC"
    )
    qc_count <- qc_count + 1
  }
}

injection_schedule <- do.call(rbind, sequence)
head(injection_schedule, 12)
```

### 3. The Confounding Diagnostic Check

Before acquiring the first sample, verify that experimental conditions are uncorrelated with technical variables:

```{r}
#| eval: false
# 1. Contingency table check across batches (want balanced cell frequencies)

# 2. Pearson correlation with run order (must be approximately 0)

```

## Simulation-Based Power Analysis

Standard analytical formulas assume normal, homoscedastic errors and ignore empirical Bayes variance moderation (`limma`). In modern proteomics and metabolomics, `limma` shrinks individual feature variances toward a pooled prior distribution, boosting statistical power for small sample sizes.

A **Monte Carlo simulation** provides the most accurate power estimation because it subjects simulated data to the exact computational pipeline used for the real study:

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true
library(limma)

simulate_ms_power <- function(n_per_group, 
                              n_features = 2000, 
                              n_de       = 100, 
                              log2fc     = 1.0, 
                              sigma_mean = 0.6, 
                              n_sim      = 50) {
  power_estimates <- numeric(n_sim)
  
  for (s in seq_len(n_sim)) {
    # Generate design matrix
    group  <- factor(rep(c("Control", "Treated"), each = n_per_group))
    design <- model.matrix(~ group)
    
    # Simulate feature standard deviations from an inverse gamma distribution
    # mimicking empirical Bayes prior distributions
    prior_df   <- 4
    feature_sd <- sqrt(sigma_mean^2 * prior_df / rchisq(n_features, df = prior_df))
    
    # Generate null feature matrix
    mat <- matrix(rnorm(n_features * 2 * n_per_group, 
                        mean = 0, 
                        sd = rep(feature_sd, 2 * n_per_group)), 
                  nrow = n_features, byrow = FALSE)
    
    # Inject biological true differences into first n_de features
    mat[1:n_de, group == "Treated"] <- mat[1:n_de, group == "Treated"] + log2fc
    
    # Fit empirical Bayes moderated linear model
    fit  <- eBayes(lmFit(mat, design))
    padj <- p.adjust(fit$p.value[, 2], method = "BH")
    
    # Calculate recovery rate of true positive features at 5% FDR
    power_estimates[s] <- mean(padj[1:n_de] < 0.05)
  }
  
  mean(power_estimates)
}

# Test across small cohort candidate sizes
sample_sizes <- c(3, 5, 8, 12)
sim_results  <- sapply(sample_sizes, simulate_ms_power, log2fc = 1.0, n_sim = 20)

data.frame(
  Replicates_Per_Group = sample_sizes,
  Estimated_Power      = round(sim_results, 3)
)
```

3. **Subtle Effect Sizes ($\Delta = 0.5$, 1.4-fold change)**: Typical of complex polygenic phenotypes, drug dose-responses, or clinical plasma biomarkers. These require $n \ge 20$ replicates per group; studies with $n = 3$ to $5$ have less than 20% power to detect these effects and will capture only extreme false-positive outliers.

## Field-Specific Sample Size Guidelines

Based on typical variance parameters observed across thousands of published Bioconductor studies, we recommend the following minimum biological replication tiers:

| Experimental System | Expected Biological SD ($\log_2$) | Recommended $n$ per Group | Justification & Caveats |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| **Immortalized Cell Lines** | 0.20 – 0.40 | **4 – 5** | Genetically identical; controlled media. Must use independent passages/seedings, not split dishes. |
| **Primary Cell Cultures** | 0.40 – 0.70 | **6 – 8** | Donor-to-donor variability introduces substantial biological heterogeneity. |
| **Inbred Animal Models (e.g., C57BL/6)** | 0.40 – 0.60 | **6 – 10** | Minimal genetic variance, but organ harvesting and feeding states introduce physiological drift. |
| **Outbred Animals / Wild-Type** | 0.70 – 1.00 | **12 – 15** | Genetic diversity inflates residual variance. |
| **Human Biofluids (Plasma, Serum, Urine)** | 0.80 – 1.40 | **30 – 100+** | Extreme demographic, dietary, and diurnal variance. Low-abundance markers suffer from high CVs. |
| **Human Biopsies (Tumor vs. Normal)** | 0.90 – 1.50 | **40 – 120+** | Tumor cellularity, necrosis, and stromal infiltration require large cohorts for robust signature discovery. |

## Pre-Acquisition Design Checklist

Before preparing reagents or submitting vials to the mass spectrometry facility, complete this verification checklist:

- [ ] **Defined Primary Contrast**: Biological comparison of interest defined with the minimal biologically meaningful fold-change explicitly specified ($\log_2\text{FC} \ge 1.0$).
- [ ] **Sample Size Justification**: Biological replicate count justified by analytical power calculation or empirical simulation based on pilot data or published variance.
- [ ] **Independent Biological Units**: Experimental units represent distinct biological individuals (no pseudoreplicates treated as independent samples).
- [ ] **Balanced Blocking**: Biological conditions are balanced across all extraction plates, digestion days, and isobaric labeling plexes.
- [ ] **Run-Order Randomization**: Complete injection sequence is randomized; correlation between condition and run order is verified to be near zero ($|r| < 0.10$).
- [ ] **Interleaved Pooled QCs**: Aliquots of a representative sample pool are scheduled every 8–10 injections to monitor retention time and intensity stability.
- [ ] **Conditioning Injections**: 3–5 initial conditioning injections of pooled matrix are scheduled before biological acquisition begins.
- [ ] **Missingness Contingency**: Sample size is inflated by $15\% - 30\%$ to ensure required power is preserved after low-abundance feature dropout.
- [ ] **Confounder Table Verified**: Contingency matrix of condition against technical factors (plate, technician, reagent lot) contains no zero cells or collinearities.

## Summary

- The experimental design decided before acquisition sets an immutable ceiling on the analytical and biological validity of your study.
- **Biological replicates** determine statistical power to generalize findings; **technical replicates** quantify instrument precision and should not be pseudoreplicated.
- **Randomization** protects against time-dependent instrument drift; **blocking** neutralizes unavoidable batch effects (plates, days, TMT plexes).
- Multiple hypothesis testing across thousands of features shifts the required per-test significance threshold by orders of magnitude ($\alpha^* \ll 0.05$), necessitating larger cohorts than classical single-gene assays.
- Incorporating empirical Bayes moderation (`limma`) via Monte Carlo simulation provides an accurate, realistic assessment of statistical power before committing laboratory resources.
